The State of San Francisco Politics

[img: ]

Time and again, our country has failed the working class at home and abroad, using its power as an empire to enforce exploitation. San Francisco today sits at the heart of the global capitalist system, as Silicon Valley has driven inequality and labor exploitation to a breaking point. We live in one of the wealthiest and most unequal cities in the world. The Democratic Party establishment has failed our city, but San Francisco’s values are alive and well in the working people of our city.

Since 2020, big money has flooded San Francisco politics at unprecedented scale, breaking spending records election after election. “Astroturf” groups like GrowSF, Neighbors for a Better San Francisco, Blueprint for a Better San Francisco, and the Abundance Network mimic genuine “grassroots” organizing while projecting a friendly, neighborly image. Behind their false front are the bankrolls of Big Tech and real estate interests. Many of the mega-donors fueling Trump’s Republican party and the controlled-opposition Democrats at the national level are keen to consolidate control over the life of our city.

Follow the money. These groups operate like venture capitalists: invest heavily now, expect outsized returns later. Their campaign spending is an investment, and they expect a return: more real estate speculation, weaker tech regulation, lower taxes on the wealthy, smaller government, a gutted social safety net, and a city that exists as a playground for the rich.

They have gained control over nearly every San Francisco’s political institution, including the Board of Supervisors, Board of Education, District Attorney’s Office, Democratic County Central Committee, and the Mayor’s Office. As a result, billionaire’s priorities guide the decisions that shape the lives of San Francisco’s working class.

[img: ]

While victory for the working class requires building a new society from the ground up, we put together this voter guide because we believe that voting is a critical part of winning reforms to make life easier for working people, and improve our organizing conditions. The superrich know this, which is why they spend so much money to influence election outcomes.

Former DSA SF Supervisor Dean Preston demonstrated what these reforms can achieve during his five years in office. He helped keep thousands of San Franciscans in their homes through a moratorium on COVID-19-related evictions, advanced the nation’s first resolution demanding a ceasefire in Gaza, and authored Proposition I in 2020, which doubled the transfer tax on luxury real estate and funded emergency rental relief, affordable housing, and social housing.

DSA SF member and Supervisor Jackie Fielder continues to hold the line against our Mayor and one of the most conservative Boards of Supervisors in decades. She is fighting budget cuts and the affordability crisis, defending San Francisco’s immigrant communities, exposing government corruption and police abuse, and has spent almost a decade organizing to establish a public bank in San Francisco.

We put out this guide because San Francisco has never seen this scale of spending on local elections, ballot measures, and PR campaigns propping up the status quo. Working people deserve to know who is trying to buy their vote, whose interests our elected officials serve, and who is fighting back.

This guide is a tool for seeing through the fog of capital and informing the organizing work necessary to build a San Francisco that works for us all.

The transformation our city needs cannot be won at the ballot box alone. It requires a collective movement capable of fundamentally changing our economy and government. But city elections shape the terrain on which we organize and, this November, San Francisco voters have a chance to shift the terrain, advance a new vision of San Francisco, and bring the continued consolidation of power in billionaire hands to an end.

Common Terms

[img: ]

Conservative / Liberal / Moderate / Neoliberal / Progressive

San Francisco politics can be confusing because different groups across the political spectrum often look and sound alike. In other parts of the country, elections are framed as a choice between “liberal” and “conservative,” with “progressive” describing the left wing of the Democratic Party. San Francisco has very few politicians who openly describe themselves as conservative or Republican. Instead, the main political divide is within the Democratic Party and those described as “progressives” versus “moderates.”

DSA is a socialist, anti-capitalist organization so we fall outside of the usual divide between “moderate” and “progressive” Democrats in San Francisco. Still, we often work alongside progressives who fight for a more equal society, even if we believe progressive ideology lacks the class analysis we believe is necessary for real progress. Moderates, by contrast, welcome the billionaire takeover of our city as they defend the interests of the wealthy and powerful against the needs of working people.

This guide will use the terms “conservative,” “moderate,” and “progressive,” even though the distinctions between these terms can be difficult to define in SF politics. We also use “liberal” and “neoliberal” to describe an ideology that puts private markets in charge of the production and distribution of life’s necessities, at the expense of the social safety net and government intervention.

[img: ]

NIMBY / YIMBY

The guide also uses the terms “YIMBY” and “NIMBY,” short for “Yes In My BackYard” and “Not In My BackYard.” In San Francisco politics, these terms are used to frame debates over housing development as a choice between two opposing sides.

Nearly all San Francisco politicians and political groups agree that the city has a housing affordability crisis and that the government needs to act. Well-funded YIMBY groups and politicians argue that deregulation of the housing market will make housing more affordable. YIMBY groups have the support of a majority of the Board of Supervisors and have held influence over the Mayor’s office for almost a decade. They believe the government should focus on facilitating, incentivizing, encouraging development while stepping aside and letting the market operate freely.

What a DSA Endorsement Means

[img: ]

One way DSA SF participates in elections is by developing, proposing, and then endorsing campaigns. Our chapter votes democratically on endorsements, choosing campaigns that advance our socialist cause, grow our movement, and demonstrate our solidarity with key allies in the fight against billionaires. An endorsement from DSA SF means a serious commitment of members’ time and effort with on-the-ground organizing, public support, and direct involvement in campaign decisions.

For the November 2026 election, we have endorsed 3 ballot campaigns: Yes on Propositions B and I, and No on Proposition D. We have also endorsed opposition to the rest of the Mayor’s Charter Power Grab, Propositions D, E, and F. In addition, we endorsed 2 candidates running for office: Bunny McFadden and Reina Tello.

This voter guide explains our analysis of candidates and ballot measures, along with our strategic recommendations for how San Franciscans should vote. Recommending a campaign does not mean we believe it directly advances socialism or agree with every decision or statement the campaign has made. It means we believe it is a step toward improving conditions for the working class.

San Francisco Board of Supervisors

District 2

In Short:

District 2 is home to San Francisco’s wealthiest enclaves, and its Supervisorial seat has launched some of the City’s most notable political careers, from Gavin Newsom to Dianne Feinstein. Historically this seat has been one of the most conservative on the Board, held for over 25 years by some of the City’s most conservative Democrats. The D2 supervisor represents some of the wealthiest, most powerful people on the planet. With such a powerful constituency and a rich history of moderate supervisors rising to prominence within the Democratic Party, the District 2 race is always one to watch. The race therefore offers a clear local example of the political and economic power that Economic Democracy seeks to challenge: DSA’s program calls for democratic control over economic power rather than allowing wealthy individuals, investors, and corporations to exercise outsized influence over public policy.

Workers Deserve More: Economic Democracy

Establish public ownership of the largest corporations and essential industries to ensure democratic control and accountability to the people. Enact aggressive wealth taxes on the richest individuals and corporations to spend on public goods and infrastructure.

District 2’s race is still technically on the ballot, but the real contest ended after the June election, when Lori Brooke dropped out after a decisive loss to Stephen Sherrill who outraised her two to one. Brooke represented a business-friendly, homeowner-aligned politics that would once have been a natural fit for this district.

Sherrill won the primary by fusing that old-money, big business tradition with a new coalition of big tech and big money YIMBY-bankrollers, reflecting the configuration currently in power at City Hall. Old money has united with big tech to form a new conservative coalition, one that pairs market deregulation with an embrace of the Police State, making Ending Mass Incarceration and Police Immunity an important part of the fight in this district. This race is also directly related to DSA’s Housing For All plank; rather than treating housing as a human right and expanding publicly owned social housing and protections for renters, the candidates rely on private development and market forces to determine who gets to live in Francisco.

Workers Deserve More: End Mass Incarceration and Police Immunity

Demilitarize police departments, disempower police unions, and redirect funding to public services as steps towards fully abolishing the police and prison system which protects the rich and jails the poor. Treat drug addiction and mental illness as health issues, not crimes. End qualified immunity and empower civilian oversight bodies with real authority over the police.

Workers Deserve More: Housing for All

Housing is a human right. Build new, publicly-owned social housing, strictly regulate investment properties, establish universal rent control, and guarantee the right to counsel for all tenants.

Stephen Sherrill

The incumbent deeply entrenched in establishment, anti-working class politics for decades.

Stephen Sherrill was a Republican until 2009 and first registered as a Democrat in 2023 and has been welcomed into the “moderate” Democratic fold. He is a second-generation private equity guy who graduated from Yale, interned in George W. Bush’s White House, and worked in Republican Mayor Michael Bloomberg’s administration in New York.

After working in the Bloomberg-funded Mayor’s Office of Innovation during Mayor London Breed’s tenure, Sherrill’s appointment to the District 2 Supervisor seat was a surprise pick. It has since become the subject of a federal corruption investigation and it is widely believed that Breed tapped Sherrill as part of a quid pro quo agreement with Michael Bloomberg. This hasn’t hurt Sherrill’s political prospects. He is a close ally of Mayor Lurie, whose Office of Innovation received a $7 million grant from Michael Bloomberg in December 2025.

Sherrill is backed by the YIMBY/Abundance coalition, earning the endorsement of GrowSF and a substantial personal donation from its founder, Garry Tan. Sherrill’s opposition to a high-rise housing development at the site of the Marina Safeway hasn’t jeopardized his support from YIMBY groups, however.

Nicholas Berg

The Republican candidate dedicated to slashing the critical government services working San Franciscans rely on.

Nicholas Berg is the San Francisco Republican Party’s choice for District 2. His main policy positions include championing charter schools at the expense of public schooling, slashing critical government services, and ending ‘Housing First’ policies. These represent the core tenets of conservative politics in the city: unlimited cruelty towards the working class in order to benefit rich capitalists. Nicholas Berg would be a disaster for the people of District 2.

District 4

In short:

Joel Engardio was the Sunset’s representative until he was recalled in the fall of 2025 – an ironic twist given Engardio’s instrumental role in earlier recalls of several Board of Education members and progressive District Attorney Chesa Boudin. During his brief stint in office, Engardio led the effort to close part of the Great Highway and turn it into a coastal park now known as the Sunset Dunes. He placed this proposal on the November 2024 ballot, where it passed with a citywide majority despite most of District 4 voting against it. Ten months later, Engardio’s tenure succumbed to the anger of his constituents over this decision. Engardio made this decision with a remarkable lack of transparency. During the recall campaign, it was revealed that Engardio had deleted a May 2024 meeting with park advocates from his private calendar, concealing it from public view. The Sunshine Task Force unanimously condemned him for this violation.

The debate over the Great Highway continues to be a flashpoint in the district and a proxy for multiple interconnected concerns around climate and environmental protections, public transit, community input in City planning, and the ubiquitous issue of affordability. These questions relate to the Green New Deal, which calls for a worker-led transition to sustainable infrastructure and major public investment in transit, as well as Democracy For All, which emphasizes protecting people’s ability to meaningfully participate in decisions that affect their communities.

Workers Deserve More: Green New Deal

Workers must lead our democratic transition to a green future. Pass a federal jobs guarantee. Implement massive investment in sustainable, publicly owned energy and transit infrastructure. Phase out the use of fossil fuels. Reclaim and repair our earth’s resources for all.

Workers Deserve More: Democracy For All

Extend full voting rights to all permanent U.S. residents and restore the right to vote for incarcerated people and people with criminal convictions. Establish statehood for Washington D.C. and recognize the right to self-determination for all U.S. territories and colonies. Expand and protect voting rights and end voter suppression.

Mayor Lurie’s first appointee to fill Engardio’s seat, Beya Alcaraz, was in office for only a week before her career succumbed to allegations surrounding the pet shop she formerly owned. Revelations of tax avoidance and labor violations, along with dozens of dead pets in freezers, forced her to resign. Lurie’s second pick has had a longer tenure: Alan Wong took office in December and then won a special election in June. He’s one of the staunchest allies of the Mayor on a Board packed with them and is considered one of the Board’s most conservative members.

The second-place finisher in the June special election, Natalie Gee, had significant labor and progressive support. After Wong’s definitive victory, Gee decided not to run again and labor has not backed any of the remaining candidates, narrowing the field to Albert Chow, Jeremy Greco, and the incumbent Wong.

There is currently no DSA backed candidate seeking election in District 4, but the district deserves a candidate who prioritizes an affordability agenda for residents of the Sunset District, celebrates the cultural diversity of the district, fights speculative luxury housing development, fights for important environmental protections, and works with local constituents to make sure that they are included in city decision making.

Albert Chow

A critic of Mayor Lurie with a focus on neighborhood-level control but also concerning connections to real estate and conservative organizations.

Albert Chow is a hardware store owner and longtime neighborhood club president who came in third place in June’s special election. His campaign gives voice to Sunset residents whose top priority is preserving neighborhood-level influence in planning decisions.

Chow opposed last year’s Family Zoning Plan, Mayor Lurie’s citywide upzoning initiative, criticizing the lack of community input on a plan that could bring buy-outs, displacement, and luxury development to the Sunset. Chow’s skepticism of top-down mandates also underpins his support of Proposition G, which would reopen the Great Highway to car traffic.

Chow has also campaigned on public safety and a stronger police presence in the Sunset, a position that is likely driven by the coalition he’s fostered, which blends new and old conservative institutions. His candidacy has been endorsed by the Chinese American Democratic Club, Connected SF, and the San Francisco Association of Realtors. Although Chow states that his primary concern is preserving community governance, his platform and campaign has the backing of real estate interests and conservative organizations.

Jeremy Greco

A working class candidate backed by some progressive organizations but lacking a robust campaign.

Jeremy Greco, a teacher and actor, came in fourth in the June special election and is a new face in the San Francisco political scene. He’s the sole working-class candidate in the race, having spent 16 years as a worker-owner at a local cooperative. Greco has endorsed DSA SF’s Proposition B, to establish a Public Bank, and Proposition I, which will secure key funding for affordable and social housing. His platform also includes progressive policies like support for affordable and social housing, investment in preventative solutions to public safety, and expanded Muni service. Citing environmental concerns, he supports keeping Sunset Dunes Park open while strengthening coastline protections.

His platform deserves consideration, but he is not viewed as a viable contender in the race. Despite his progressive platform, his campaign has not generated significant labor support, even after labor-supported candidate Natalie Gee dropped out in June and subsequently endorsed him.

Greco is endorsed by the Harvey Milk LGBTQ+ Democratic Club, the San Francisco Tenants Union, and former District 4 Supervisor Gordon Mar, but he has little institutional backing and has only raised about $6,000, all from small-dollar individual donors.

Alan Wong

An unwavering ally of Mayor Lurie who abandoned former progressive positions to attain power and the backing of big money.

Alan Wong is the incumbent supervisor and is running for his first full term after being appointed by Daniel Lurie in December 2025 and winning the June special election to serve out the remainder of ousted Supervisor Joel Engardio’s term.

You may remember Alan Wong from his campaigns for City College Board of Trustees, where he ran as a strong progressive candidate with the backing of AFT 2121 (the City College faculty union) and a broad coalition of progressive support.

You’d be forgiven for thinking that Alan Wong was a different politician from the Supervisor now campaigning against Proposition B (DSA SF’s endorsed effort to establish a Public Bank) and serving as one of Mayor Daniel Lurie’s closest allies on the Board. But the warning signs were there. Wong had already built a record around public safety and policing, including co-founding Stop Crime SF, a group that advocates for increased police resources.

Political opportunist is the perfect term for Supervisor Wong, who now campaigns as Mayor Lurie’s “partner” on the Board and has the backing of the Lurie coalition’s massive campaign war chest. During his June special election against progressive Natalie Gee, Wong received nearly $1 million from PACs tied to Lurie and his billionaire allies.

During his time on the Board, Wong has consistently voted with the conservative wing of the Board, even co-sponsoring Matt Dorsey’s abstinence-only permanent supportive housing bill, which would evict some of the City’s poorest residents onto the streets if they use drugs in their homes.

In addition, Wong’s vote against placing the DSA SF endorsed Proposition B on the ballot has aligned him with ConnectedSF, a conservative political group led by former Republican Marie Hurabiell, whose transphobia is so egregious even her ally Daniel Lurie had to condemn her tweets. Wong shared the stage with Hurabiell recently at a rally organized as a counter protest to our Public Bank campaign launch, held just around the block.

Wong’s vision for the City is indistinguishable from that of Mayor Lurie: increasing funding for SFPD, deregulation of real estate, and offering vague statements of support for LGBTQ and immigrant communities while cutting their safety net funding.

District 6

In Short:

Incumbent Matt Dorsey will likely win reelection, but we would be remiss not to weigh in on his odious stances. Dorsey represents a district with some of the city’s most reactionary pockets, particularly among SoMa residents whose politics have been shaped by increased poverty and homelessness in their neighborhood.

The combination of the 2022 redistricting, which removed the Tenderloin neighborhood from District 6, ongoing luxury development and the high concentration of corporate tech offices has further shifted the political makeup of the district to the right. D6 was formerly represented by Jane Kim, a staunch progressive notwithstanding her support for the infamous Twitter Tax break. The Supervisor before Dorsey, Matt Haney, was elected as a progressive in 2018, but pivoted to the center to embrace pro-developer politics as he used the seat as a launchpad for State Assembly.

Supervisor Dorsey is one of the right-most Board members, espousing a carceral, pro-corporate, and overtly Zionist politics that obsessively demonizes the left. But we should not assume that Dorsey carries unanimous support from his own neighborhood. Many of the same neighbors now demanding more policing and arrests of people who use drugs once formed an unlikely alliance with prison abolitionists and leftists in 2018 to successfully oppose the construction of a new jail in the district.

Dorsey’s embrace of a new War on Drugs is directly in opposition to DSA’s values of Ending Mass Incarceration and Police Immunity, we fight for demilitarizing police, redirecting resources toward public services, treating addiction and mental illness as health issues rather than crimes, and moving toward the abolition of the capitalist carceral system. Our opposition to Supervisor Dorsey is also rooted in our platform tenets: Free Palestine and End The U.S. War Machine, as Dorsey has used his political platform to defend US support for Israel’s brutal genocide in Gaza.

Workers Deserve More: End Mass Incarceration and Police Immunity

Demilitarize police departments, disempower police unions, and redirect funding to public services as steps towards fully abolishing the police and prison system which protects the rich and jails the poor. Treat drug addiction and mental illness as health issues, not crimes. End qualified immunity and empower civilian oversight bodies with real authority over the police.

Workers Deserve More: Free Palestine

Recognize the rights of the Palestinian people, including the right of return, the right to resist military occupation, and the right to self determination in a free Palestine with Jerusalem as its capital. End all military and economic aid to Israel. Prosecute U.S. and Israeli leaders responsible for the genocide in Gaza.

Workers Deserve More: End the U.S. War Machine

Defund the Department of War. End all foreign wars and close overseas military bases. Support and fully employ veterans. End immunity for U.S. war criminals and support the International Criminal Court’s mandate to prosecute war crimes.

Today, these very neighbors could again form a big tent around opposition to the RESET Center, a detention facility for people suspected of being intoxicated or under the influence. The model is a reboot of the “drunk tank,” where people are held until they are deemed sober and risk re-arrest if they try to leave before then. San Francisco abandoned this model more than 75 years ago because it was ineffective and dangerous, with many people dying in custody. Today, that same model is being lauded by the Mayor and the Board as an innovative new approach, and Supervisor Dorsey considers it the “most important drug policy development since the advent of fentanyl here in San Francisco”.

Growing pushback is emerging, from people who could be directly impacted by the Center, to neighbors who view it as an attempt to skirt existing restrictions and effectively create a new jail in their neighborhood. That fight gives Healthcare For All and End Mass Incarceration and Police Immunity a concrete expression in D6: whether people struggling with addiction are met with healthcare, housing, and harm-reduction services, or with detention and arrest.

Workers Deserve More: Healthcare for All

Guarantee universal healthcare at no cost to individuals, including complete access to reproductive and gender-affirming care. Expand healthcare access and quality by investing in new public hospitals and clinics. Make all medical education and training public and free.

Workers Deserve More: End Mass Incarceration and Police Immunity

Demilitarize police departments, disempower police unions, and redirect funding to public services as steps towards fully abolishing the police and prison system which protects the rich and jails the poor. Treat drug addiction and mental illness as health issues, not crimes. End qualified immunity and empower civilian oversight bodies with real authority over the police.

These disparate groups have a common aim today, just as they did in 2018. There are deep reactionary currents in the neighborhood, but they are being harnessed by big money political forces and turned against their poorer neighbors and their neighbors in supportive housing. Dorsey thrives by pitting these groups against each other, turning neighbors who could otherwise find common ground into opponents. However, grassroots organizing to stop the ineffective and cruel RESET Center might see a larger pushback of the conservative politics Dorsey espouses and the start of a better era for District 6.

Matt Dorsey

The anti-DSA, pro-Zionist incumbent hellbent on imposing cruelty against our neighbors struggling with addiction.

Matt Dorsey is the incumbent supervisor in District 6. Mayor London Breed appointed him to the seat in May 2022 after Matt Haney was elected to State Assembly. Before entering elected office, Dorsey served as Head of Communications for the San Francisco Police Department and worked as a lobbyist for Lyft and Veritas, a major corporate landlord.

Since taking office, Dorsey has established himself as the one of the rightmost figures on the Board. He earned an F in our 2025 Report Card and has been a consistent ally of Mayor Lurie’s mass-arrest, mass-jailing agenda. But Dorsey’s carceral approach predates Lurie. In 2022, he proposed a plan for the city to make at least 100 arrests per night of people who use drugs.

Dorsey has been in recovery from addiction and is an openly gay man living with HIV. He has used that lived experience to embrace a “Recovery First” approach that prioritizes abstinence from substances as a prerequisite for access to housing and other harm-reductive services. His position aligns him with organizations like the Salvation Army, the right-wing Cicero Institute and Drug Free Sidewalks SF that have pushed back against “Housing First” models.

These groups advocate against syringe and safe supply access and other services rooted in harm reduction, and have helped push the City away from the statewide “Housing First” approach, an evidence-based framework which recognizes that people experiencing homelessness and struggling with addiction often cannot begin recovery without first having stable housing and their basic needs met. Dorsey earlier this year spearheaded legislation which barred the city from funding any new supportive housing that will not evict residents for using drugs. This demonstrates the cruel and counter-productive nature of his “Recovery First” agenda, which actively prevents people from accessing the housing they need.

Matt Dorsey is also an ardent Zionist and proud supporter of Israel (see his favorite tweet opener “as a non-Jewish Zionist…”). In January 2024, he voted against DSA member and then Supervisor Dean Preston’s resolution calling for a ceasefire in Gaza. This past Pride weekend, he took to Twitter/X to ask Elon Musk’s AI chatbot ‘Grok’ to elaborate on his justifications of the ongoing genocide including “@grok, which side in the Hamas-Israeli War is the most pro-LGBTQ+? 🇮🇱” and calling Palestinians “suicidally motivated civilians.” He also said that protestors wearing keffiyehs were cosplaying as “Jihadist terrorists who routinely torture and kill queers.”

It is difficult to overstate how much Matt Dorsey hates DSA, calling us “politically repellent and anti-semitic” while calling on the Democratic party to crush the rise of socialism. His opposition to DSA is not incidental to his politics; it is core to his public identity and his reactionary platform.

That is exactly why we cannot let his attacks define the terms of the fight. We need to keep organizing, keep building our power, and keep showing that the politics Dorsey wants to crush are the politics that will make reactionaries like him a bitter memory. He can keep attacking us. We will keep getting stronger.

Alex Behrend

A political outsider with strong values but lacking the organizational support to win the race.

Alex Behrend was a latecomer to the District 6 Supervisor race and is Matt Dorsey’s sole challenger. Behrend presents a bold, working class-forward vision for the city: he is one of the few candidates on the San Francisco ballot to support divesting from Israel and industries involved with apartheid and genocide against the Palestinian people, he wants to protect worker rights by guaranteeing the right to legal counsel, and supports DSA SF’s endorsed Proposition I, which will secure key funding for affordable and social housing. His platform serves as a direct counter to Matt Dorsey’s conservatism, but his campaign lacks the momentum to truly take on the big money’s darling. His late entry and lack of key endorsements suggest a nonviable campaign, but perhaps an opportunity for a meaningful protest vote in a race considered to be already decided.

District 8

In Short:

The Castro was once known as the progressive stronghold that launched Harvey Milk, Harry Britt (the first Democratic Socialist elected in SF), and generations of progressive queer leaders. Over the years, due to gentrification and rising housing costs, the district’s voter base has shifted right, leading to the supervisorial terms of prominent moderates Scott Wiener and Rafael Mandelman. Mayor Lurie has partnered with Supervisor Mandelman to ride a wave of moderate momentum in San Francisco, but as a working class affordability crisis drives socialist electoral wins across the country, this race has also demonstrated a shifting Overton window in the city’s “swing” district, with three progressive candidates on one side and one lone “moderate” on the other, before the moderate pick dropped out. District 8 is a battleground over Housing For All: rising housing costs and gentrification have reshaped the district, while candidates increasingly recognize that affordability requires more than market-rate development and must include tenant protections, social housing, and public investment.

Workers Deserve More: Housing for All

Housing is a human right. Build new, publicly-owned social housing, strictly regulate investment properties, establish universal rent control, and guarantee the right to counsel for all tenants.

Even amid the big money takeover of our local government, District 8 candidates are scrambling to position themselves as representatives of the working class and promising to elevate progressive issues like public transit and housing affordability. Most of the candidates espouse YIMBY-aligned views on new housing development but there is a strong emphasis on tenant protections and publicly-funded solutions like a public bank and social housing. The District 8 race is a lesson in the contested boundary between “progressive” and “moderate” wings of the local Democratic Party. The race speaks directly to DSA’s demand for Economic Democracy. Debates over a public bank, social housing, and the influence of real estate and tech money all come down to a central question: who controls our resources and shapes our city, the public or private capital?

Public Defender

In Short:

It is hard to talk about the Public Defender’s office without first talking about current San Francisco District Attorney Brooke Jenkins. Jenkins was appointed to the office after a campaign spearheaded by corporate interests and conservatives succeeded in recalling Chesa Boudin in 2022. Boudin led efforts to soften the disproportionate violence that the legal system levels against minority groups and the working class in general. He moved to end cash bail and end the “three strikes” law, which escalates punishment for lower level crimes. Jenkins worked in Boudin’s office until she joined the campaign to remove him. Her departure came after Boudin reached a plea agreement that prevented her from continuing to prosecute a mentally ill man whom Boudin’s office found not guilty by reason of insanity. She was paid handsomely to work on the recall campaign, which was backed by the big money group, Neighbors for a Better San Francisco.

Jenkins has leaned into “tough on crime” politics endemic to the Republican Party, while maintaining a “liberal” facade. The result has been San Francisco, a supposedly “progressive” city, embracing some of the most draconian law enforcement attitudes in decades. County jails are rapidly filling and, in many cases, pushing the jail system past its breaking point. Black and Brown folks continue to be disproportionately targeted by the criminal legal system. Police surveillance technology is flourishing, while legal protections for immigrants are deteriorating.

For many of the working class and marginalized people caught up in this system, a public defender is their only access to legal protection. As criminal cases have surged, the Public Defender’s Office has faced workloads it says have become unsustainable, with the office reporting a nearly 65% increase in cases filed by the District Attorney since 2019.

The District Attorney’s aggressive charging practices and SFPD’s mass arrests, paired with poor jail conditions, has pushed the Public Defender’s Office to the brink. But Mano Raju, the current Public Defender running for reelection, has made the fight against overcriminalization a central part of his tenure, including challenging excessive caseloads, defending clients’ constitutional rights, challenging police misconduct and abuse, backing immigrants victimized by ICE while SFPD stands by, and supporting the women in San Francisco jails speaking out against rampant sexual misconduct.

Manohar Raju

A bulwark against Lurie, DA Jenkins, and their billionaire allies’ agenda for mass incarceration and arrests.

Mano Raju is running unopposed for San Francisco Public Defender. He has served in the office since 2019, having been appointed by London Breed and winning reelection twice. Raju has faced the difficult task of steering the Public Defender’s Office through surging case numbers and consistent budget cuts, leaving his staff overburdened. In May 2025, Raju took a stand against the City’s efforts to mass arrest and overcharge, arguing that the resulting Public Defender caseloads were pushing the entire criminal justice system to the brink of collapse. The Public Defender began declaring unavailability and refusing to take on additional cases without additional staff.

This stand demonstrates the power of an independently elected Defender to use the full authority of the office to protect their clients, their staff, and the people of San Francisco. Raju is using his powers to their fullest extent in order to protect his clients, and his fight will have national implications if he succeeds in court.

Every additional arrest generated by Mayor Lurie’s agenda, and every case pursued rather than diverted before trial, creates another person who must receive constitutionally adequate representation. Mayor Lurie has continued former Mayor Breed’s trend of increasing funding for the District Attorney and SFPD while simultaneously failing to adequately fund the Public Defender’s office, even as arrests and prosecutions have skyrocketed.

The fight over the quality of representation working class people deserve points to a larger contradiction in the criminal legal system: the City can dramatically expand its capacity to arrest and prosecute people, without an equivalent expansion of its capacity to defend them. When one side of the system grows unchecked, the constitutional right to counsel becomes increasingly difficult to fulfill.

The Public Defender’s independence is particularly important, this office is one of the few places within the criminal legal system whose institutional purpose is to stand with the person facing prosecution rather than the government bringing the case. For working class people and marginalized communities, that role can mean the difference between having someone fight for their rights and being processed through an increasingly punitive system with little recourse.

It can feel like we are moving backwards toward the era of mass incarceration and the failed War on Drugs, but this race is an opportunity to remember that San Francisco still has people willing to hold the line. We can be proud to have a Public Defender willing to use the power of his office to fight for us.

The Conservatives

Phil Kim

Phil Kim won re-election to the School Board in the June election after running and losing in 2016 and 2018. Proposition B

Establishes the structure for a San Francisco Public Bank, a future funding mechanism for affordable housing that would keep San Franciscans’ money in the city and out of the hands of the big banks.

Proposition B would authorize the establishment of a city-owned Public Bank, establishing its governance and guardrails with appointments distributed across the Board of Supervisors, Mayor, and other City departments. This structure is intended to prevent control of the bank from being concentrated in any one office or elected official. The measure also outlines the mission and lending philosophy of the bank: local control, community wealth building, public welfare, and more. It also specifies to avoid industries that have negative socioeconomic or environmental outcomes, including predatory lending, fossil fuels, prisons, firearms, and tobacco. These reforms represent steps toward Economic Democracy, a foundational change necessary to truly move past capitalism.

Workers Deserve More: Economic Democracy

Establish public ownership of the largest corporations and essential industries to ensure democratic control and accountability to the people. Enact aggressive wealth taxes on the richest individuals and corporations to spend on public goods and infrastructure.

In addition to DSA San Francisco, Proposition B has support from a broad range of labor and progressive organizations, including SEIU 1021, the San Francisco Labor Council, and the United Educators of San Francisco. It has also attracted support from political figures further to the right, including the SF Democratic Party, Matt Dorsey, and Bilal Mahmood. The limited opposition includes Mayor Daniel Lurie, Supervisors Stephen Sherrill and Alan Wong, and organizations including ConnectedSF.

From Jackie Fielder and community organizers co-founding the SF Public Bank Coalition in 2017 alongside DSA SF, to the establishment of the Reinvestment Working Group under then Supervisor Dean Preston, to multiple resolutions from the Board of Supervisors, the process of creating a Public Bank in SF has been a decade in the making. If Proposition B passes, San Francisco would take a major step towards making history as the first American city with a municipal public bank, although the first public bank in the US, the Bank of North Dakota, was founded by socialists in 1919.

Banks are one of the most durable mechanisms through which wealth is accumulated and concentrated in the hands of a few. They pool enormous amounts of capital, from the collective wealth of the richest people in the world to the life savings of the working class. Despite being free from public accountability or control, they wield immense power to shape the direction of global industries and markets, ensuring that money keeps flowing upward.

In contrast, a Public Bank places that same power in the hands of the working class, keeping public wealth under public control and allowing for democratic decisionmaking to determine the way it is used. Prop B establishes such a bank for San Francisco, and paves the way for the city to stop relying on Wall Street banks to fund public development and local environmental projects. It provides a pathway to divest public funds from fossil fuels, private prisons, and Israeli genocide.

Proposition E is part of a larger package of Charter Amendments which seek to centralize power within the Mayor’s administration while undermining the ability of working-class San Franciscans to shape city government. A single campaign committee supporting all three measures has raised over $17 million, mostly from a handful of billionaires including Chris Larsen, Michael Mortiz, and John Pritzker.

Proposition E empowers the City Administrator, a somewhat obscure position which is appointed by and controlled by the Mayor. If the measure were to pass, it would extend the City Administrator’s term from five years to 10 years, give the City Administrator the authority to propose laws around contracting, raise the threshold for which contracts need to be voted on by the Board of Supervisors, from $10 million to $25 million, and put the City Administrator in charge of the city’s technology and capital projects.

The purpose of this is simple: move more and more power away from the citizen-elected members of the Board of Supervisors and into the hands of an office directly controlled by the Mayor. The current Mayor, along with every previous Mayor since at least 1992, have been servants of capital enabling successive waves of gentrification & displacement, and consolidating more power under the mayor’s office is a clear attack against our values of A Real Democracy.

Proposition G

An attempt to reopen the Great Highway after its close in 2024 that represents a years-long fight between those who want the stretch of land to remain a park and those who want it to go back to being a highway.

Proposition G would reopen the portion of Upper Great Highway that is now known as Sunset Dunes Park to private motor vehicles on weekdays, while keeping it closed to vehicles on weekends and holidays. This would largely be a return to how this stretch of land operated from 2021 to 2025 (called the “compromise”), where the roads were closed from Fridays at noon to Mondays at 4:00 AM. However, G would close the stretch to cars later on Fridays, starting at 6PM rather than noon. This is the third time San Franciscans will be voting on this issue, with 2022’s Prop I (unsuccessful) seeking to undo the “compromise” and allow cars full-time and 2024’s Prop K (successful) converting the piece of road into a full-time park.

Prop G was put on the ballot by Great Highway for Everyone, a group of Sunset residents, motorists, and others who had many issues with Sunset Dunes, including perceived worsening traffic and lack of community involvement in the process. A similar coalition led the recall of Joel Engardio, after he misled constituents on the campaign trail, claiming to support the “compromise”.

The money is on the side of Save Sunset Dunes, the committee formed to oppose Prop G. They’ve raised more than $750,000 in 2026 from environmental groups, SF progressives, and big tech elites – with the bulk of funds coming from a handful of major tech donors including Yelp CEO Jeremy Stoppelman, compared to the just $101,000 that Great Highway for Everyone has raised.

The amount of money for the pro-park side adds to the feeling that this park is being imposed on Sunset residents by outsiders rather than done with the community. However, the No on G coalition is much more diverse than the Yes on G campaign would have you believe. It includes moderates like Scott Wiener and Stephen Sherrill, and those further left like former Supervisors Eric Mar, DSA SF member Dean Preston, and DSA SF Socialist in Office Jackie Fielder. Most unions have not taken a position on this issue, besides SEIU 1021 which endorsed No on G. Many progressive groups have opted out of taking a side on the divisive issue, including the League of Pissed Off Voters and the Harvey Milk LGBTQ Democratic Club.

This reflects that the working class is deeply divided on this issue. Due to decades of car-centric planning, the majority of Sunset District (and many other parts of SF) rely on cars to get around: for work, for school, for leisure. District 4 has among the highest private vehicle mode shares in the city, with around 76% of trips currently happening by car. Fast, reliable public transit is severely lacking on the West Side, and these material realities determine how and why different segments of the working class are in such disagreement about this piece of public land.

There are real ecological benefits to closing this space to cars, including the decreased pollution, noise and artificial lighting, the regeneration of local fauna and flora to a diverse ecosystem that has been systematically degenerated in the past, and of course the actual utility of a public recreational space by the beach increasing physical and mental health for residents. Excessive automobile use is not only detrimental to locals, but also disproportionately harms people in the Global South due to climate change, resource extraction, and wars over oil.

Segments of the American working class depend on automobiles, so the socialist answer should not be to dismiss these concerns, but to work with the communities impacted to demand safe, fast, free, reliable public transit across the West Side and the rest of the City. Similarly, fears of real estate speculation and gentrification associated with the park conversion must be taken seriously and channeled through organizing for social housing, tenant unions, and the decoupling of land from the private speculative market.

These contradictions speak to the need for real solutions to the real problems surfaced by this proposition. Prop G sits at the intersection of the real estate development interests of the past and present contending with serious environmental consequences. Ultimately, only radical transformations like A Green New Deal that address the root causes of these contradictions can resolve these issues. Capitalists and their political representatives will only present band-aids that pit the working class against itself. In stark contrast, DSA organizes for socialism to truly offer the Freedom to Flourish for all people.

Proposition I

A rededication of existing funding for affordable and social housing after the Mayor’s repeated attempts to slash it amid a skyrocketing affordability crisis.

Proposition I would permanently dedicate funding from the sales of real estate worth over $10M (like skyscrapers and megamansions) to affordable and social housing production, acquisition and rehabilitation of affordable housing, tenant stabilization, and eviction defense programs. This measure would also exempt new multifamily housing construction from the tax.

This revenue comes from 2020’s DSA-endorsed transfer tax increase (coincidentally also called Prop I). While this original measure in 2020 technically went to the general fund due to legal limitations, it was accompanied by a unanimous Board of Supervisors resolution clarifying that this revenue was intended to be used on rent relief, affordable housing, and similar programs. Since then, moderate Mayors Breed and Lurie have taken advantage of this discrepancy between the letter of the law and spirit of the law and have refused to spend the majority of the money as the voters intended.

In 2023, new legal precedent allowed cities to dedicate revenue from transfer taxes to specific purposes like affordable housing. In February of 2026, Supervisor Bilal Mahmood and Mayor Daniel Lurie moved to undo Prop I by slashing the transfer tax. This effort to cut taxes for the wealthy has since been shelved, in part due to DSA SF’s letter writing campaign and the momentum on Prop I, but its moderate proponents have maintained that they would still like to cut this tax in the future. Crucially, this means that this measure does not take away from other priorities: the revenue either goes towards affordable and social housing as voters intended, or it will be taken away entirely.

Prop I has earned strong support from a broad swath of groups, including SEIU 1021, IFPTE Local 21, United Educators of SF, AFT 2121, SF Tenants Union, and the Sierra Club. The measure is opposed by Mayor Daniel Lurie, the SF Police Officers Association, SF Firefighters Local 798, SF Building & Construction Trades, SF Chamber of Commerce, SF YIMBY, and the SF Taxpayer’s Association.

Some of this opposition makes sense, like Lurie and the Chamber of Commerce, who put the interests of capital and private profit above human need, and thus want to be able to cut the tax. But one would think that YIMBY, an organization whose entire premise is supposedly building housing, would support Prop I and funding affordable housing production. Instead, they’ve sided with corporate real estate interests, arguing that this tax on ultrawealthy real estate speculators should be slashed even further in order to spur market rate housing production, ignoring the new housing exemption, and the fact that the vast majority of the revenue comes from large commercial real estate transactions.

As socialists, we believe housing is a human right; Proposition I would create permanently affordable, mixed-income social housing by using existing taxes on the wealthy speculators that are directly fueling our housing crisis. While one ballot measure is not enough to win the world we deserve, Prop I would be a huge step in the right direction in making San Francisco a place everyone can live. We plan to continue this fight long past this election, but we can’t do it alone. Check out our Social Housing and Tenant Organizing Working Groups to get fight for Housing for All!

Regional Transit Measure

A measure that delivers key funding to BART just in time to prevent service cuts but unfortunately places that cost unfairly on the working class.

A “Yes” vote on this measure supports a modest increase in sales tax across the 5 counties served by BART; a “No” vote will result in major service cuts across all above transit agencies, 15 closed BART stations, and worsened traffic.

This is a necessary, crucial tax that is vital to sustaining Bay Area public transit. However, this is also a “regressive” tax, placing a higher proportional burden on working people to pay for this tax. Since this measure would tax purchases rather than income, and because working-class households spend a greater share of their income on necessities than high-income households, this measure is asking for workers to pay more relative to what they earn, rather than asking more from the wealthy or corporations. Funding a universally beneficial good like public transit should not be a burden put onto working people.

Though we recommend voting for this measure, this measure is a lesson about the political power that drives these different measures forward. SB 1031, the predecessor to SB 63 which put this measure on the ballot, originally included a payroll tax on employers, which would have been a progressive tax put upon companies that also heighten demand for public transit. Capital had the organizing capacity to kill that bill, and despite organization and support from the SF Board of Supervisors, California YIMBY, and Housing Action Coalition, it could not be saved.

We need substantial change in funding public transit so that it does not fall on the working class to bail out our public transit every few years. A socialist San Francisco means real solutions like a Green New Deal that guarantee free, reliable, climate-positive transit for everyone.

The State Propositions

Proposition 1

A bond that would deliver key funding to housing across the state at a time when housing prices are skyrocketing.

Proposition 1, The Veteran and Affordable Housing Bond Act of 2026, is a bond measure that would authorize $11.25 billion in State borrowing to finance a range of housing programs across California. As the name suggests, a portion of the funding would support home loan programs for veterans, low income senior housing, homeownership loans for low/mid income households, farm worker housing, and student housing.

California is facing a severe housing affordability crisis as rents continue to rise, pushing people out of cities, and in some cases, California altogether. Bonds are one of the ways state and local governments can finance major housing investments in response to these long term needs. A bond is essentially a loan to the government: the government borrows money upfront and repays it over time, with interest. For voters, the question is therefore not simply how much money a bond raises, but what the government uses that money for and whether the long-term public benefit justifies the cost of repayment.

We would rather fund housing through taxes on the rich than bonds which further enrich already-wealthy lenders through interest payments. However, in a housing crisis like California’s, a bond that seeks to prioritize the housing needs of the state’s most marginalized communities is worth holding our noses and voting for.

A proposition that would allow more of California’s tax revenue to sit in the General Fund, protecting us from market instability but failing to meaningfully challenge systemic issues that cause the instability in the first place.

A proposition that would allow more of California’s tax revenue to sit in the General Fund, protecting us from market instability but failing to meaningfully challenge systemic issues that cause the instability in the first place.

Supporters argue that a larger reserve would make it easier to protect public services during recessions and economic downturns. With a smaller reserve, they say, the state could run out of funding during emergencies and face pressure to cut services or raise taxes.

The measure has drawn little organized campaigning from either side. Governor Gavin Newsom and the California Democratic Party support it, while conservative group Reform California and several Republican lawmakers oppose it.

From a socialist perspective, Proposition 2 is primarily about how the state manages its finances rather than directly improving people’s material conditions. At the local level, progressive advocates have fought back against needlessly letting public funds sit in reserves rather than go to work on our streets, but at the state level, the impacts are much fuzzier. A larger reserve could help protect social services during economic downturns, but it does not address the underlying inequality that leaves working people vulnerable in the first place.

Proposition 5

Changes how statewide recalls work by preventing a successor from being elected on the same ballot as the recall, a structure that significantly increased the possibility of a right-wing governor during the recall attempt against Governor Newsom.

Proposition 5 would change how recalls of California state officials work. Currently, if the threshold is met for a recall attempt, a special election is held immediately and voters are asked 1) whether the official should be removed from office and, if they are removed, 2) who should replace them. Prop 5 would remove the second question. Instead, the office would be filled through the existing appointment process, which generally means a separate special election to decide who will replace the recalled official.

The support or opposition is largely split along party lines, with the Democratic Party and Democratic lawmakers supporting and Republican lawmakers and conservative groups opposing. Supporters argue that having the successor elected at the same time as a recall causes confusion at the ballot box: voters aren’t sure who to vote for if they don’t support a recall, allowing for a fringe candidate to have a real chance at winning.

Part of the DSA’s platform is the strengthening and securing of democratic values (it’s why it’s in our name!). Our current electoral system, one that punishes independent political movements in favor of strengthening the two party system that is failing us, is one that needs significant changing. However, the broader question is what role recalls should play in a democratic system.

Republicans attempted to use this tool to oust Governor Gavin Newsom in 2021 over COVID policies and sanctuary state laws, a recall that failed significantly but would have put Republican Larry Elder in the seat if it had been successful.

Fringe, far right candidates like Elder shouldn’t have such easy access to our State government. This measure will help prevent a disaster like Elder, while we keep our eyes on preventing ongoing corporate Democrat disasters like Gavin Newsom.

Proposition 41

An attempt by moneyed interests to stop Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax, by burdening tax measures with new pre-election audits.

Proposition 41 would require pre-election audits for tax-related ballot initiatives, require special taxes to undergo regular audits, and prohibit state taxes from exceeding a state spending limit. A “special tax” is a tax that can only be used to fund specific purposes, such as Prop 40’s billionaire tax which would specifically fund healthcare, food assistance, and public education.

This ballot measure, alongside Proposition 42, was designed in opposition to Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax. If Proposition 41 receives more yes votes than Proposition 40, Proposition 40 could be prevented from taking effect if a court determines that the two measures conflict.

California’s ultra-wealthy, billionaire tech CEOs, and venture capitalists, oppose Prop 40 and rather than accepting meager amounts of wealth redistribution to support California, they have dedicated significant resources to opposing Prop 40, both through campaigning and the creation of two opposing propositions.

DSA SF’s Recommendation: Vote No on 41

Proposition 42

A second attempt by the capitalist class to stop Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax, by prohibiting certain new taxes from being created and retroactively limiting existing taxes.

Proposition 42 would amend the California Constitution to prohibit new state taxes on retirement accounts, individual assets, or personal savings, as well as retroactively limiting existing taxes.

Prop 42, alongside Proposition 41, was designed in opposition to Proposition 40, The Billionaire Tax, as a “poison pill” that could kill the Billionaire tax. If Prop 42 receives more yes votes than Prop 40, Prop 40 could be blocked from taking effect if a court finds the measures conflict. Even if Prop 40 takes effect, Prop 42 would restrict the state’s ability to impose similar taxes on financial assets in the future.

A “yes” on Proposition 42 will not protect your retirement account. It will just further protect California’s billionaires and allow for the further deterioration of our vital social services.

DSA SF’s Recommendation: Vote No on 42

Proposition 43

An attempt to raise the threshold to pass new special taxes from a simple majority to two-thirds, severely hurting the ability to pass measures like DSA SF’s Proposition I in the future.

Proposition 43 would raise the threshold of passage for voter-initiated local taxes from a simple majority to a two-thirds. Special taxes are taxes dedicated to a specific purpose, such as funding housing, transit, or healthcare. Under current law, local governments already need two-thirds approval for special taxes they place on the ballot, but recent court decisions have allowed voter-initiated special taxes to pass with a majority.

This would have direct implications for San Francisco, where some of our most impactful programs have been funded through voter-initiated special taxes, including the 2018 universal childcare measure and the 2018 homelessness tax on large businesses that funds housing and homelessness services.

The class conflict is clear: the measure is backed by a statewide coalition that includes real estate and business interests, while the taxes affected by the higher threshold are among the tools local governments use to fund public services.

If Prop 43 passes, it could make it harder to secure material gains through local ballot measures by making new dedicated revenue sources more difficult to pass. Municipal taxation alone cannot achieve socialism, but local revenue remains an important tool for winning concrete improvements in people’s lives.

DSA SF’s Recommendation: Vote No on 43

Proposition 44

A complicated issue around how much local healthcare clinics must spend directly on care that pits organized labor against healthcare advocates.

Proposition 44 would require non-profit health centers to spend at least 90% of their annual revenue on patient care and program services.

The measure is primarily being pushed by SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West, a healthcare worker union representing over 120,000 healthcare workers and allies. They claim Prop 44 will ensure more clinic revenue flows to patient services, rather than clinic executive salaries.

Prop 44 also cannot be discussed without the context of 2024’s California Prop 34, a measure that also applied a higher required patient care spending rate. However, the bill was written to only target the AIDS Healthcare Foundation, a group that both provided care and did positive political advocacy around rent control and affordable housing. The increase in care spending slashed their advocacy budget, revealing the true purpose behind the millions of dollars spent by real estate interests.

The result is a very complicated situation for socialists. On one hand, you have organized labor pushing for money to be moved away from executive salaries. On the other hand, you have a mechanism that has been used to punish positive political advocacy in the past being applied again in a way that many groups claim will hurt our public healthcare infrastructure. Given the competing interests at play, we do not have a recommendation on Prop 44.

DSA SF’s Recommendation: No Recommendation

Proposition 45

A measure to slash environmental reviews on new development projects that will benefit building developers and real estate interests far more than housing advocates.

Proposition 45 would make a number of changes to the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) to speed up environmental review for housing, transportation, clean energy, education, and health-related projects.

This measure is funded by a variety of real estate and business interests, including the California Building Industry Association, California Chamber of Commerce, California Association of Realtors, and Prologis (Amazon’s primary landlord). The proponents point to examples of NIMBYs utilizing CEQA to delay projects over noise and aesthetics, which subsequently leads to higher costs and less projects “penciling out.” Opponents say that Prop 45 would weaken the ability for communities to have input on polluting projects, allowing wealthy corporations to pass environmental costs onto communities and working people.

It is clear why organized business and real estate would put this on the ballot: they are utilizing real fears about rising housing costs to launder another environmental deregulation measure that would pad corporate profits. We are seeing communities across the country and across California come together to fight against AI data centers, which are the physical embodiment of putting profit over human need. Studies show that these resource-guzzling facilities spike local energy costs, worsen air quality, and are disproportionately found in marginalized communities.

This measure would weaken the working class’s ability to fight against corporate greed in their communities, like data centers. As socialists, we know that CEQA, Prop 45, or any other law will not be sufficient. We believe in democratic control of our communities and a worker-led transition to a green future. This does not mean we freeze our communities in amber, but that we fight for a city where growth and changes benefit working people, rather than just than the 1%.

DSA SF’s Recommendation: Vote No on 45
