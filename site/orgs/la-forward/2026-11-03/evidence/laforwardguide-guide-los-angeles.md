## los-angeles / Citywide offices

### Mayor: Nithya Raman [Endorsed]

LA Forward members determine who we endorse in every race and their choice in this race was decisive. More than 90% of our members voted to endorse Nithya Raman as the best option for Mayor.

Being LA’s Mayor is an incredibly difficult job in the best of times. And needless to say, these are not the best of times. As former Deputy Mayor and Deputy Controller Rick Cole has noted, “beneath the broken sidewalks is a broken system that desperately needs fixing.”

Of the two candidates in this race, we believe Councilmember Raman is best suited to ensure LA City government truly delivers for all Angelenos.

Mayor Bass is smart, hard-working, and rooted in a long history of organizing and public service. That’s why we were proud to endorse her in 2022. But with notable exceptions such as verbally standing up to ICE, she hasn’t risen to the challenge of being Mayor, especially when it comes to managing our city government and communicating effectively with the public. Bass has frequently taken the political path of least resistance, taking for granted the progressive voter base that got her elected initially. Bass has prioritized appeasing powerful interests like the police union and Airbnb, which are the same forces that have funded conservative candidates across the city, and are now spending millions of dollars to boost her re-election campaign.

Despite Raman’s much shorter tenure in local politics, she has a clearer understanding of the ineffective areas of our city government, and a vision for how to fix them.

Raman’s track record in office over the last six years has largely aligned with LA Forward’s priorities

* Nithya helped move LA toward a citywide unarmed crisis response system

* Nithya spearheaded new protections for renters (even before new progressive colleagues joined her on council in 2022),

* Nithya co-authored the sanctuary city ordinance to protect immigrants

* Nithya championed good governance reforms to reduce corruption and increase the effectiveness of government

* Nithya secured a unanimous council vote for building electrification climate policy, and backed expanded transportation and mobility options

There is a clear contrast between the two candidates over the last four years

* Nithya used her position as chair of the Council’s Housing and Homelessness Committee to try to get the Venice Dell affordable housing project out of the red tape purgatory to which Mayor Bass, City Attorney Hydee Feldstein Soto, and Councilmember Traci Park had consigned it.

* Nithya was one of only a few votes against deals created by Mayor Bass that drained the city’s coffers through overspending on police salaries and a Convention Center overhaul, leading to cutbacks of other vital city services.

* Nithya fought for real charter reform, including council expansion, greater oversight powers for the City Controller, and increased police accountability, while Mayor Bass unnecessarily delayed the Charter Commission’s start by eight months and then thwarted attempts at broader reform.

* Nithya used her role on the AQMD board to fight climate change and air pollution before Mayor Bass removed her for being too hard-charging.

Aside from all the issues where we’ve appreciated Raman’s leadership more than Bass’s, we are most hopeful about things that are not policy-specific.

Recently Bass supporters have been spreading the idea that LA City’s mayor is “weak” and that the Council has all the real power. While the Council wields tremendous power, the Mayor has extensive authority to make change within city government, as long-time nonpartisan observers have noted in recent pieces by Robert Greene and Jim Newton.

We need a Mayor who pays attention to management, hires top leaders who understand how to get things done at City Hall, and communicates in a way that meets the challenges of the always-on media landscape.

We need a Mayor who is laser-focused on ensuring LA City government works well on a daily basis, holds Department leaders accountable for outcomes, and keeps policy implementation and Angelenos’ experience of government top of mind.

This is not an easy task for anyone but Bass has been largely missing in action and we see Raman asking the right questions and setting the right priorities.

We’re also impressed by Raman’s ambitious and detailed policy agenda. We’re reassured by the commitments she’s made to us personally for collaborative governance and coalition-building, including on issues where we don’t agree.

While we appreciate Bass’ focus on homelessness, we agree with Raman’s assessments about the flaws of “Inside Safe” which is getting more expensive and less effective. And while we appreciate some of the achievements that have occurred during Bass’ tenure in office, like getting LA off of coal power, in most cases, Bass is taking credit for policies that others spearheaded long before she was Mayor. Bass was not the first mayor to notice and address homelessness despite what she has claimed. Eric Garcetti, for all his own flaws, spent real political capital to campaign for more funding for homeless housing with Measure HHH in 2016 and work to get bridge/interim housing built across the city.

Bass’ appointment of Jim McDonnell as police chief has been a huge step backwards for the city, a stance so dismaying that it caused the Black LA Young Democrats to support Raman.

Raman has received an impressive array of endorsements including City Controller Kenneth Mejia, former Controller Laura Chick, the California Working Families Party, Eastside Padres, Latino Coalition of Los Angeles, ACCE, Initiate Justice Action, nearly all environmental environmental organizations – Sierra Club, LA League of Conservation Voters, Sunrise Movement, Food & Water Action, Climate Action California, California Environmental Voters –, a growing number of Democratic clubs, plus unions of healthcare workers (SEIU 121RN Nurse Alliance, Committee of Interns and Residents/SEIU), transportation workers (Transportation Communications Union/IAM, Transport Workers Union of America), hospitality workers (UNITE HERE 11), LA City professional workers (EAA Union) Writers Guild of America West, Carpenters, Inlandboatmen’s Union of Pacific (ILWU), AFT Local 1521A, and UAW Region 6.

For all these reasons and more, LA Forward is proud to join partners in endorsing Nithya Raman for Mayor.

### City Attorney: Marissa Roy [Endorsed]

In the race for LA City Attorney, voters have one of the easiest choices on the ballot. Marissa Roy is the best choice for LA City Attorney. After a disastrous four years of incumbent Hydee Feldstein Soto, Roy promises to make the office of City Attorney a true public interest law firm that protects workers, tenants, immigrants, and communities from corporate abuse — not a rubber stamp for the powerful. We endorse Marissa Roy for LA City Attorney.

The City Attorney is LA’s lawyer: they advise the mayor and City Council, prosecute misdemeanors, and decide how aggressively the city takes on wage theft, illegal evictions, consumer fraud, and environmental harms. In practice, that means the City Attorney can either use the law to hold slumlords, union‑busting employers, polluters, and abusive police accountable – or duck those fights and leave communities on their own.

Recent years have shown how much damage a cautious, corporate‑friendly City Attorney can do, from weak enforcement against bad landlords to settlements that protect city departments more than residents. A progressive, movement‑aligned City Attorney can instead shift the office toward impact litigation, civil rights enforcement, and real accountability for abuses that fuel homelessness, displacement, and distrust.

Marissa Roy is a lifelong Angeleno who decided to go into public service when Proposition 8 stripped marriage equality from LGBTQ Californians, including her younger brother. She began her legal career in the LA City Attorney’s Office, bringing major workers’ rights cases to protect people from wage theft and other exploitation. Roy later served as outside counsel for Los Angeles County, where she joined legal actions against the Trump administration to defend sanctuary policies, DACA recipients, students, and immigrants seeking asylum. Today she is a Deputy Attorney General in the California Department of Justice, suing large tech companies over practices that threaten public health and safety and standing up for tenants who are wrongfully evicted and consumers who are defrauded.

Roy is explicit: she is running to transform the City Attorney’s Office into the largest public interest law firm in Los Angeles. Her agenda includes:

* Protecting workers and tenants by aggressively enforcing wage and hour laws, cracking down on wage theft, and defending renters from illegal evictions and abusive landlord practices.

* Taking on corporate and tech abuse by scaling up impact litigation against corporations engaged in unlawful, unfair, or deceptive conduct that harms Angelenos.

* Making civil rights and immigrants’ rights central to the office’s mandate, including pushing back on racial profiling, unconstitutional policing, and ongoing attacks from the Trump administration.

* Pursuing smart public safety by prioritizing strategies that actually make communities safer — diversion, prevention, accountability — rather than reflexive criminalization that feeds mass incarceration.

Roy’s entire career has been about using the law as a tool for economic and social justice, not as a shield for powerful institutions.

The incumbent, Hydee Feldstein Soto, came in third during the primary so Roy will be up against John McKinney, the second-place finisher.

McKinney, a deputy district attorney, comes from a traditional “tough on crime” background and has run on a more carceral, prosecutor‑first approach that would exacerbate the very problems progressives are trying to solve. He’s been backed by the Republican Party, the LAPPL police union, Republican DA Nathan Hochman, and a whopping $3 million of outside spending from Airbnb during the primary alone.

John McKinney’s right-wing ties are deeply disturbing. His campaign is backed by some of the biggest Republican and MAGA donors in California. And his campaign is being run by Republican consultants who have come under scrutiny for unethical conduct while working for Trump, including a controversial fundraiser accused of shady practices like money laundering.

Roy stands alone as the candidate who is both rooted in Los Angeles and clearly committed to turning the office loose on wage thieves, slumlords, polluters, and abusive corporations – and to reorienting the City Attorney’s work around workers, tenants, and over‑policed communities instead of City Hall insiders.

## los-angeles / Ballot measures

### Measure EE — City Administration Charter Amendment [Oppose]

Measure EE is a set of amendments to the LA City Charter proposed by LA City Council ostensibly related to Ethics. With a couple notable exceptions (e.g. Measure LA, PRK), LA City Council ditched most of the substantive amendments proposed by the Charter Commission on the theory that they required more study. More study was not apparently required for a set of ideas that the Commission had not even proposed but which Councilmembers themselves favored. First and foremost, reducing the number of full City Council meetings required by the Charter from three times per week to once a week. There’s nothing inherently wrong with reducing the charter requirements for meeting frequency, but we find it deeply disappointing that the Council chose to advance this little-studied reform with no accompanying reforms would promote greater public access to Council deliberations

EE also would increase the number of signatures needed for a ballot referendum by 50%. Currently petitioners must collect a number of signatures equal to 10% of the votes cast in the last mayoral election to qualify a referendum. Amendment EE would increase this to 15% of ballots cast. Proponents say this makes the number of signatures needed for referendums and voter initiatives the same. Opponents note that with initiatives signature gatherers have four months, while with a referendum they have only one. The effect of this provision is to make referenda basically impossible. A referendum is when voters want to overturn a policy just passed by the City Council, whereas an initiative is a new policy.

While in theory this change is a bad thing, in practice, the referendum has been used mostly by corporate interests to undermine pro-worker policies passed by the council. The most recent example is when business groups attempted to overturn the Olympic wage ($30/hr for tourism workers), with ample financing from the airline and hotel industries.

EE also includes several provisions that function more as feel-good sweeteners for voters than solutions to real problems.

Specifically, the measure will enable independent inspectors for the Los Angeles Police Department and Fire Department more power even over the objections of the LAPD and LAFD commissions (there’s not a history of commissions objecting) and will ban alums of the City Ethics Commission from running for local office for five years after leaving office (this hasn’t been problem).

Measure EE is likely to pass but we recommend you vote No to send a message to City Council to get serious and stop kicking the bucket down the road when it comes to real reform for good government. We’re sick and tired of it.

### Measure FD — Sales Tax to Fund the Fire Department [Support]

Measure FD would add 0.5% sales tax in the City of Los Angeles, generating about $345 million annually for the Los Angeles Fire Department. The money could fund firefighters, paramedics, fire engines, ambulances, fire station construction and repairs, communication systems, and wildfire preparedness. The tax would remain in place until voters repeal it.

Because Los Angeles County voters approved Measure ER in June 2026, the City's sales tax is already scheduled to increase from 9.75% to 10.25% on October 1st. Measure FD would add another half percentage point, bringing the rate to 10.75% if approved and implemented.

LA recommends a YES vote on Measure FD. Asking working-class Angelenos to absorb another increase deserves careful consideration and scrutiny. However, the City of Los Angeles has significant unmet fire and emergency-response needs, and Measure FD creates a dedicated funding source with a strong requirement for maintenance-of-effort, annual audits, and public oversight.

Why is Measure FD on the ballot?

Measure FD originated as a voter initiative spearheaded by the United Firefighters of Los Angeles City Local 112 (UFLAC), which represents nearly 3,400 firefighters. The union began gathering signatures in January 2026. The union argues that Los Angeles Fire Department staff, facilities, and equipment have failed to keep pace with population growth and emergency call volume. After supporters submitted enough valid signatures, the City Council voted 14-0 in June to place the measure on the November ballot.

Supporters argue that Los Angeles has roughly the same number of firefighters today as it did decades ago while annual emergency calls have increased dramatically. The campaign says LAFD had 3,370 firefighters in 1965 compared with approximately 3,387 today, while the City has grown substantially and annual calls for services have increased roughly fivefold.

The January 2025 Palisades Fire and the June 2026 Lineage Fire have also heightened public attention to LAFD staffing and preparedness. More staffing cannot replace accountability for deployment, management, or command decisions. But major fires and other recent emergencies have strengthened the argument that Los Angeles needs greater emergency-response capacity.

What would the money pay for?

Measure FD is a special tax, meaning the revenue must be spent on LAFD functions rather than deposited into the City’s unrestricted General Fund.

The ordinance allows funding for additional firefighters, paramedics and civilian personnel, fire engines, trucks, ambulances, aircraft and helicopters, firefighting and rescue requirements, new and upgraded fire stations and emergency medical service facilities; emergency communications; and brush clearance and wildfire readiness. The City would deposit revenue into a dedicated account and keep it available exclusively for authorized uses

The City must continue providing LAFD at least the average percentage of discretionary budget funding that the department received during the 10 fiscal years preceding implementation. If the City fails to meet that requirement in a fiscal year, it cannot levy the Measure FD tax that year. The provision is explicitly designed to ensure that new revenue supplements rather than replaces existing LAFD expenditures.

Measure FD also requires an annual independent financial audit and creates a five-member citizens’ oversight committee. The Mayor, Fire Chief, City Controller, City Administrative Officer, and Chair of the City Council committee overseeing public safety would appoint committee members. The committee would review whether Measure FD revenue was spent for legally authorized purposes and report publicly at least annually. However, the committee would be advisory only, and would not determine spending priorities, manage projects, or oversee other sources of LAFD funding. The Mayor and City Council would also review and approve reporting and audits.

Who supports Measure FD?

The campaign is organized through Repair, Rebuild, Respond - Fund LA Fire Now, sponsored by UFLAC which is the union of LA City Firefighters. The campaign identifies its top funders as UFLAC, the State Building and Construction Trades Council of California, and the law firm of McNicholas & McNicholas LLP.

Mayor Bass has supported the initiative, and the City Council voted 14-0 to place it on the ballot. The Central City Association, representing downtown business interests, has supported the measure.

Some supporters have clear institutional interests. Firefighters would benefit from expanded staffing and departmental resources, while construction unions could benefit from new station construction and modernization projects. The initiative requires new and replacement fire-station projects funded by Measure FD to operate under the City’s applicable project labor agreement and prevailing-wage requirements, while also permitting workforce-development programs aimed at City residents. LA Forward believes the public would be better served by additional firefighters, functioning stations, and modern emergency equipment.

Who opposes Measure FD?

While the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association is opposing, the group spending the most money against FD is the police union, the LA Police Protective League. We think they’re wrong about most things so that doesn’t give us concern.

We take more seriously the opposition argument that sales taxes are regressive. Increasing the sales tax disproportionately burdens residents who can least afford higher everyday costs, and fire protection should instead be prioritized within the City’s existing budget. Opponents also argue that Measure FD has no end date. The ordinance states that the tax remains in effect until voters repeal it. Opponents ask whether every underfunded public service should be funded by increasing consumption taxes rather than other revenue sources.

How should progressives think about Measure FD?

A progressive revenue system should place more of the burden on those with the greatest ability to pay. A sales tax does the opposite: households that spend a larger portion of their income on consumption bear a disproportionately large burden, but that’s not the debate.

The practical choice is whether to approve this funding mechanism or continue relying primarily on annual City budgets, which have failed to address long-term staffing, facilities, and equipment needs. Measure FD includes safeguards, such as making the revenue available only to LAFD, prohibiting supplementation of existing City support, requiring expenditure audits, and revoking the City's authority to levy the tax if it violates the maintenance-of-effort requirement.

Measure FD is not an ideal revenue measure. But increasing wildfires, especially in the wake of the Palisades and Lineage Fires, and the ever-present threat of earthquakes require Angelenos to address longstanding emergency-response infrastructure needs now rather than when the next disaster hits.

LA Forward suggests a YES vote on Measure FD.

### Measure LA — Comprehensive Infrastructure Planning Charter Amendment [Support]

LA Forward endorses LA City Charter Amendment LA.

Measure LA comes out of the comprehensive Charter reform process Los Angeles undertook over the past year, the first major examination of the City Charter in decades. Across numerous public meetings, there was widespread support for several of the changes now packaged under Measure LA, especially those on long-term infrastructure planning and reforms to the City’s budgeting process. The final package of reform was placed on the ballot by the City Council on a 13–0 vote, with two members absent.

Los Angeles Needs to Plan Beyond One Year

Los Angeles maintains an enormous network of streets, sidewalks, parks, bridges, buildings, and other public infrastructure. Historically, however, the City has not been required to produce a citywide Capital Infrastructure Plan (CIP), making it harder to prioritize projects across departments. The Bass Administration recently released Los Angeles's first citywide CIP, an important step toward more coordinated infrastructure planning. But the first iteration has also generated critiques about both the process used to develop it and the usefulness of the final product. We want long-term capital planning to become embedded in the City's culture, improve over time, and continue regardless of who occupies the Mayor's Office. Measure LA would put that expectation into the Charter by requiring the City to regularly produce a Capital Infrastructure Plan and move Los Angeles toward a two-year budget cycle, while retaining annual budget actions and updates.

Clear Responsibility for Public Works

Measure LA would also restructure how the Department of Public Works is managed. Today, authority is divided among the Board of Public Works, Director of Public Works, and the heads of the Department's bureaus. Measure LA would give the Director greater responsibility for managing construction projects, administering contracts, granting street permits, and recommending Public Works budgets. While the Board of Public Works is not eliminated and retains important responsibilities, Measure LA also gives the City Council the ability to modify or eliminate the Board in the future through an ordinance.

Modernizing How the City Uses Its Money and Assets

Measure LA would also update several longstanding Charter provisions governing City finances and contracting. It would remove Charter prohibitions on a) mortgaging City-owned property (Charter Section 104a) and b) the City engaging in purely commercial or industrial enterprises without voter approval (Charter Section 104g). As an article in the new publication, L.A. Material notes, LA is one of very few cities in the U.S. with these provisions. It’s led to absurd situations like the City Council needing to ask voter approval for starting a snack stand at the Greek Theatre. In removing these restrictions, Measure LA provides additional flexibility and creative financing mechanisms to our City leaders, which can be crucial in addressing the City’s most pressing concerns.

What It Will Cost

The City Administrative Officer estimates that establishing the two-year budget process and Capital Infrastructure Plan would require approximately $4 million to $6 million in one-time costs, but expects these ongoing costs will be absorbed by reorganizing existing resources. LA Forward believes this tradeoff is imperative so that our City can build the processes necessary to plan and manage our investments effectively.

Measure LA is championed by LA City Controller Kenneth Mejia, former controller Ron Galperin, current and former council budget chairs Katy Yaroslavsky and Bob Blumenfield, LA Forward, Streets For All, and by the nonprofit Investing In Place, led by Jessica Meaney, which has truly led the thinking around the need for a comprehensive plan for spending our infrastructure dollars.

We urge you to vote Yes on Measure LA.

### Measure PL — City Planning Charter Amendment [Support]

If enacted, this Measure PL would implement reforms proposed by the City Charter Commission to the City’s land use process.

The biggest change would involve the dissolution of the City’s Area Planning Commissions – these seven regional commissions are the decision making bodies for small projects in their designated region and handle appeals of decisions made by the City Planning Department staff. Instead, a new Neighborhood Appeals Commission would take their place. This new Commission would have seven members and would consider appeals of decisions made by the City’s Planning Department staff. The City would continue to have a City Planning Commission, the body that considers large scale projects proposed within the City.

The measure would also amend the process for making changes to the City’s General Plan by reducing the wait time before the proposed amendment is adopted. The measure would give the City Council 75 days to consider any proposed amendment that was recommended for approval by the City Planning Commission and the Mayor; if the City Council does not act in 75 days, the proposed amendment would be adopted.

The measure would also allow the Department of Planning to appoint staff to review, investigate, and hold hearings for discretionary land use applications. Currently, only staff in the Office of Zoning Administration can conduct these reviews; this measure would allow additional staff to be appointed to decrease application processing periods.

Overall, the measure will likely decrease application processing periods because of the additional staff that can review and hear applications - however, it should be noted that consolidating the seven Area Planning Commissions into a single Neighborhood Planning Commission may introduce a new bottleneck into the process (if an appeal is filed). But, having seven different bodies hear land use applications also runs the risk of uneven development practices in different parts of the City - a single appeals body will provide a uniform voice to (hopefully) promote equitable land use practices across the City.

### Measure PRK — Parks Funding Charter Amendment [Support]

LA Forward endorses LA City Charter Amendment PRK.

Los Angeles loves our parks, but over the last decade we’ve seen them decline with funding cuts. Park facilities need almost $3 billion in repairs, and pools, recreation, and senior centers have cut hours.

The reason is chronic underfunding. LA City parks are funded two-thirds less than similar cities. Park funding hasn’t kept up with other City services. We’ve lost one-third of the full-time park staff needed to keep parks safe and clean.

Measure PRK is a once-in-a-generation opportunity to transform our parks system for working families across Los Angeles – restoring clean beaches, safe playgrounds and facilities, expanding recreational and community programming that keeps kids and seniors connected, safe and active, and ensuring every Angeleno has a welcoming place to gather with family and friends, build community, and stay cool.

Measure PRK does this by increasing the parks guaranteed budget over 10 years, so our parks can give Angelenos the services they need.

Currently the City Charter requires that the parks department receive .0325% of the total assessed value of taxable properties in Los Angeles, which means $303 million for the park system. In ten years, that allocation would grow to approximately $493 million. If PRK passes, the allocation would double to $986 million by 2036.

Measure PRK would help fund a variety of critical services Angelenos rely on without raising taxes, including:

-Restoring public hours and programs at the City's 120+ recreation centers, senior centers, museums, and historic sites - making sure they can stay open 7 days a week

### Measure PRT — Airport & Port Charter Amendment [Support]

Measure PRT is a proposed amendment to the City of LA Charter regarding governance of the Harbor and Airports Departments.

The Departments of Airports, Harbor, and Water and Power are known as Proprietary Departments within the City of Los Angeles. Each of these departments is controlled and managed by a board of commissioners. These departments are governed by the Charter, so any changes to the governance, structure or powers of these departments must be accomplished by Charter amendment as opposed to city council action. This ballot measure contains a few modest reforms to the existing Charter language concerning these departments.

The first proposal concerns the makeup of the Board of Airport Commissioners. The Board currently consists of seven members, with one member residing “within the area surrounding Los Angeles International Airport" and one member residing “within the area surrounding Van Nuys airport.” This proposal would require that three members live near LAX and two members live near Van Nuys airport, thereby giving these residents majority control over the board. Those “areas” are defined by ordinance of the city council. The Commission would remain a seven-member board. We appreciate the idea of making the Commission especially sensitive to the needs of people directly impacted by the noise and air pollution generated by the airports. Airports affect the entire region as well, so we’ll need to monitor how the Commission operates with new makeup to ensure the change in composition isn’t having negative consequences.

The second proposal adds a section to the Charter specifying how the Harbor Board manages and funds its public use assets. The Harbor Board controls the assets within the Harbor District for the public’s benefit. The Charter requires that some amount of the water frontage is reserved for public use. This new section creates a Public Asset Investment Plan and codifies the existing annual practice of allocating 10% of the operating income of the Harbor Department to fund waterfront public access projects.

The third proposal involves the approval process for leases and property development within the Harbor District. The Harbor Board controls the development of the Harbor District and may purchase or lease various assets consistent with the department’s purpose. This proposal would give the Board the authority to require applicants seeking a lease, a lease extension, or to develop certain property within the Harbor District to provide an assessment of the economic impact, including the estimated workforce effects, on the Los Angeles region. Small businesses and non-profits may be exempt from this requirement.

Finally, the measure streamlines for all departments the maximum length of lease terms. Each of these departments controls and manages certain property and has the power to enter into leases consistent with the department’s purpose. Currently, leases may be entered into for a period of 30 years or up to a maximum of 50 years with city council approval. But for the Harbor Department alone, the city council may approve a lease up to a maximum of 66 years. There is no explanation for this disparity. Thus, the proposed language allows the city council to also approve maximum 66-year leases for the departments of Airport and Water and Power to align with the Harbor Department.

We recommend you support this measure. These are modest changes to streamline and strengthen these departments’ operations. Codifying an existing operational policy that has shown to be effective, like the Harbor District’s annual allocation of funds to public access projects, is a good government measure to ensure adherence.

### Measure TE — Palisades Transfer Tax Exemption [Oppose]

Measure TE would create a temporary exemption from Los Angeles’s Measure ULA real estate transfer tax for certain properties damaged or destroyed in the January 2025 Palisades Fire and sold within five years of the fire.

Why is Measure TE on the ballot?

Measure ULA, approved by Los Angeles voters in 2022, imposes an additional transfer tax on high-value real estate transactions and dedicates the revenue to affordable housing, homelessness prevention, rental assistance, eviction defense, and related programs. ULA has generated nearly $1.2 billion from 1,633 transactions through April 30, 2026.

Following the January 2025 Palisades Fire, city officials began considering whether homeowners whose properties were destroyed or seriously damaged should have to pay the ULA tax if they sold rather than rebuilt. Councilmember Traci Park, whose district includes Pacific Palisades, became the leading advocate for an exemption. The proposal was introduced with Councilmember Imelda Padilla and seconded by Councilmember Bob Blumenfield.

The City Attorney concluded that the City Council could not create the exemption itself because its authority to amend the voter-approved Measure ULA is limited. As a result, voters must amend the measure.

The Council initially voted 15-0 to advance the proposal. The final ballot action passed 13-1, with Councilmember Eunisses Hernandez opposed and Councilmember Curren Price absent.

What do supporters say?

Supporters argue that the measure provides basic disaster relief. Some Palisades homeowners face inadequate insurance proceeds, rebuilding requirements, lengthy permitting processes, or personal circumstances that make rebuilding unrealistic. Applying an additional transfer tax when they sell damaged property could further reduce the resources they have available to recover.

Councilmember Park emphasized that the exemption is limited: it applies to residential property damaged or destroyed in the Palisades Fire, only to the original owner and first qualifying transaction, and expires five years after the fire.

Political support has been broad. The Council initially unanimously supported moving the measure forward, and nearly every member supported its final placement on the ballot. As of early August, Park had applied to author the official argument in favor, while no applicant had sought to submit an official argument for Measure TE.

What are the concerns and who is opposing?

The most significant criticism is that the exemption may be broader than necessary.

Councilmember Eunisses Hernandez opposed the measure because it was not limited to primary residences. She argued that the relief should focus on people who lost the homes they lived in rather than potentially benefiting investors, owners of multiple properties, or wealthier property owners with greater financial resources. Indeed, the City Clerk, in the summary of Measure TE, writes, “The exemption would apply to all residential properties, whether or not owner-occupied, that are sold by the owner or owners who are also the owner or owners at the time the residential property was damaged or destroyed by the Palisades Fire.” Indeed, people with properties worth less than $5 million are not affected at all, so by definition this is tax relief for those with more assets than most Angelenos can even dream of having.

Additionally, the City Clerk leaves the term “residential property” vague, though the reason differs from that of Councilmember Hernandez. The City Clerk explains that “‘Residential property’ is left undefined in the ballot text to afford the City the ability to apply a broad interpretation (emphasis added) to avoid the inadvertent exclusion of eligible transfers or properties.”

There is also a financial cost. The Los Angeles Housing Department estimated that a broader residential exemption related to the Palisades Fire could reduce ULA revenues by about 6% ($32 million), while a narrower exemption involving destroyed single-family properties was estimated at 2%, or roughly $8 million.

LA Forward joins with Councilmember Hernandez in opposing Measure TE as an overly broad exemption that is not only restricted to people who lost their primary family home and that opens the door to further attempts to cut ULA and reduce this key source of revenue for homelessness prevention.

### Measure SC — Charter Amendment: Campaign Finance [Support]

Measure SC is a relatively minor charter amendment that clarifies campaign finance rules for LAUSD board candidates and aligns those rules with rules for LA City Mayor. It pertains to rules that cover campaign contribution limits, filing deadlines, fundraising windows, and public disclosures.

There are no organized campaigns for or against the charter amendment. It’s backed by Diego Andrades, a former member of the city’s Charter Reform Commission.
