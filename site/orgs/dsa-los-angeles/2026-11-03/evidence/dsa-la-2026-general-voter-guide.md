### How to Read this Guide

DSA-LA endorsements require a membership vote and come with a commitment of chapter time and resources. Recommendations are editorial calls that may reflect a range of circumstances. Depending on the race, a label appears as a badge on the candidate's card or as a stamped result block opening the writeup. Here's what the labels look like:

🌹 Endorsed

Requires a membership vote. The chapter commits time and resources to the campaign.

👍 Recommended

An editorial pick. The candidate may not be a socialist, but we believe elements of their platform will materially benefit the working class of Los Angeles, or that a tactical vote for them is valuable harm reduction against a worse candidate who could win. Recommendations come in shades: some are enthusiastic, some are protest votes, and some simply mark the best viable option. The writeup is the only way to tell them apart.

Opposed

An active opposition. The writeup lays out the case against this candidate; the vote is still yours.

Vote YES

or

Vote NO

The chapter’s yes-or-no position on a ballot measure. The writeup explains the reasoning.

No recommendation

“No recommendation” is still a decision: we explain why we didn’t take a side, and you make your own call.

If you know voters across the state who need help, send them this same link: we’ve integrated California DSA’s recommendations and endorsements right here.

## Los Angeles City

### Mayor


Nithya Raman

🌹 Endorsed

The Mayor of Los Angeles is the most powerful figure in Los Angeles municipal politics, proposing budgets, creating executive commissions and committees, and issuing emergency ordinances, and most importantly, getting blamed for everything that goes wrong. Despite being the figurehead for city government, the LA mayor is still significantly structurally weaker [https://thelandmag.com/voter-guide/why-do-city-council-members-have-so-much-power-los-angeles/#:~:text=But%20the%20mayoral%20office%20in%20the%20city,cast%20a%20more%20important%20vote%20this%20election.] than other big-city mayors like Zohran Mamdani in New York City. That being said, the mayor has a significant amount of power over the function—or dysfunction—of the city.

And speaking of dysfunction, let’s start with the incumbent, Karen Bass.

Four years ago, the former-leftist and longtime pretty-solid-if-imperfect Congresswoman cruised to election against former Republican and very evil billionaire Rick Caruso, building a powerful mandate for her signature “Inside Safe” initiative. The initiative poured hundreds of millions of dollars into getting unhoused people off the street into temporary shelters–sometimes to permanent housing, but most importantly to Bass’ base, out of sight. Like most of Mayor Bass’ mayoralty, it’s been a political failure. While some districts, like (notably, socialist-governed) CD13 and CD4 (keep that number in mind, it comes back soon) saw vast decreases in homelessness, in districts governed by law-and-order councilmembers, it yielded continuous human rights violations against the unhoused. At a citywide level, 40% of people enrolled in the program ended up back on the street [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-04-05/under-la-mayors-300-million-homeless-program-40-have-returned-to-street], a massive expenditure of public funds, primarily into the pockets of hotel and motel operators, and allegations of corruption and embezzlement by service providers [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-09-16/la-homeless-services-federal-charges].

At some point, whatever part of her character that enabled her to be a relatively okay congresswoman stopped working, and Mayor Bass’s first term has been an endless parade of embarrassments. Polling indicates that her reputation took the largest hit in 2025 in the immediate wake of the Los Angeles wildfires, for which she was in [https://abc7.com/post/mayor-karen-bass-admits-ghana-trip-before-wildfires-was-mistake/15929179/]itially absent [https://abc7.com/post/mayor-karen-bass-admits-ghana-trip-before-wildfires-was-mistake/15929179/]; having learned little from this disaster, earlier this year Bass downplayed [https://www.facebook.com/streetsforall/posts/after-the-lineage-fire-broke-out-mayor-bass-said-the-toxic-smoke-was-not-dangero/1572033391633366/] the effects of the Lineage fire on Boyle Heights and East LA, claiming that the fumes from 85 million pounds of frozen meat burning in an ammonia fire were “safe to breathe.” Not so [https://laist.com/news/climate-environment/residents-were-told-the-air-around-lineage-was-not-dangerous-a-new-report-found-health-concerns].

Her office has suffered a number of other baffling scandals, from the deputy mayor who called in a bomb threat on City Hall [https://abc7.com/post/former-la-deputy-mayor-brian-williams-sentenced-probation-5000-fine-fake-bomb-threat-city-hall/17961420/], to her attempts to kill the Metro K Line extension [https://lapublicpress.org/2026/03/la-metro-board-bass-k-line/], to a federal probe [https://www.cbsnews.com/losangeles/news/federal-judge-questions-la-mayor-karen-bass-over-homeless-audit/] over the one audit that was conducted of LA’s homelessness services—for which Bass was, again, absent [https://www.latimes.com/politics/story/2026-09-15/bass-skips-hearing-on-la-homeless-spending-calling-probe-politically-motivated]. Not since Traveling Sam Yorty have we seen a mayor so devoted to being anywhere but here.

[img alt="Trump and Karen Bass hold a “Palisades” sign and a gavel in the Oval Office" src=/media/general/mayor-palisades-sign.webp]

Maybe this is understandable when we look at what Bass’s austerity plan has done to our city. As Democratic Socialists, DSA-LA clashed with Mayor Bass on her steadfast support to raise salaries for the Los Angeles Police Department, whose budget now makes up 46% of the city’s unrestricted budget, despite warnings that this will require drastic cuts to city services. Mayor Bass’s negotiated salary raise for cops in 2023, which DSA-LA opposed [https://dsa-la.org/dsa-la-statement-on-lappl-vote/], directly led to the city’s massive billion-dollar budget deficit that resulted in the city cutting street services and laying off hundreds of public workers. Bass has mainly tried to resolve this through quick fixes, like sneaking the Airbnb-sponsored Vacation Rental Ordinance [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-04-29/la-considers-expanding-airbnb-style-short-term-vacation-rentals] into the budget in 2026 in exchange for a $50 million tax prepayment by Airbnb [https://lamaterial.com/p/los-angeles-budget-bed-tax-airbnb-home-sharing]—which would have added 31,000 new deregulated short-term rentals on top of the 9,000 we already have, raising rents and displacing communities. Though this effort was shot down (partially thanks to DSA-LA’s own Eunisses Hernandez) Bass was rewarded anyway; Airbnb committed $1.5 million to support her re-election [https://www.saje.net/airbnb-is-spending-millions-to-shape-los-angeles-politics/].

Despite her significant problems, Bass’ campaign has overwhelming institutional backing across the gamut of the LA Power Brokers: she is endorsed by the Los Angeles County Federation of Labor (LA Fed), and the Business Federation (BizFed); she’s also endorsed by the LA Chamber of Commerce, the LA County Democratic Party, and a majority of overlapping Congresspeople. Her major donors are basically a rogues’ gallery of DSA-LA archenemies: the Los Angeles Police Protective League, Airbnb, Douglass Emmett Properties, the Central City Association, and others.

Faced with a challenger to her left picking up steam, Mayor Bass has fallen back on trying to portray herself as an “activist fighting for change,” [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-09-20/seeking-reelection-la-mayor-bass-calls-herself-an-activist-fighting-for-change] hoping people will simply forget that she’s been mayor for the last four years. The most iconic moment of this pivot was her attempt to copy New York City mayor (and DSA darling) Zohran Mamdani’s pothole repair videos, in which the city repairs a pothole in a day. In Bass’s staged version, [https://www.instagram.com/reel/DcAC8oiRFc8/?hl=en] she explains that they actually couldn’t fix the pothole because someone parked over it; then, she puts up a no parking sign and wanders into a local business. This raises many questions outside the scope of this voter guide—for example, why not just choose a… different pothole?—but it helps lead inexorably to the same conclusion: though Karen Bass may have done some admirable activism in the past, she has fully betrayed her leftist roots and become a vessel for the same broken, reactionary status quo she once sought to fight.

Speaking of fixing the broken status quo, Bass’s opponent, Los Angeles City Councilwoman Nithya Raman from the 4th district (Los Feliz, some of Silverlake, the Hollywood Hills, Sherman Oaks, Van Nuys, and Encino). Raman was the first DSA-LA endorsed elected to win her seat, and is running to Bass’s left on a platform that centers some of the most immediate material crises in LA right now: the housing crisis, our crumbling infrastructure, and the ever-present moral stain of the homelessness crisis. That’s why DSA-LA has proudly endorsed her for mayor.

Councilmember Raman, who has been endorsed by DSA-LA twice for her city council runs and once for mayoral, made significant waves with her last-minute decision to enter the mayoral race [https://lamaterial.com/p/five-days-that-blew-up-the-l-a-mayoral-race]. Raman’s first election and terms on council has been undeniably transformative in LA politics, uniting a core constituency of young and middle-aged multiracial renters dissatisfied with the city’s neoliberal status quo.

She has been a consistent champion for renters and immigrants, leading the city’s Housing and Homelessness Committee to advance the largest expansion of tenants rights in LA history in 2023, followed by supporting a major democratic socialist priority in rewriting the city’s rent stabilization laws to reduce allowable rent increases [https://cd4.lacity.gov/initiative/renters-rights/], while also maintaining a consistent position against homelessness sweeps, a position that has earned her significant hate from law-and-order reactionaries. [https://www.instagram.com/reels/DXz3P_AAK89/] And that approach has worked: while overall the city of LA saw an increase in homelessness between 2025 and 2026, in Nithya’s district it dropped 49% [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-07-24/street-homelessness-went-up-in-los-angeles-in-nithya-ramans-district-it-was-nearly-cut-in-half] because this council office pioneered an effective homelessness outreach team starting in 2021 that treated unhoused people, got them into permanent housing, and back on their feet.

While Bass takes money from Airbnb, Raman has been one of the strongest advocate [https://cd4.lacity.gov/illegal-homesharing/]s [https://cd4.lacity.gov/illegal-homesharing/] against illegal short-term rentals [https://finance.yahoo.com/news/l-considers-short-term-rental-144516585.html] (STRs) which remove homes from the housing supply and increase rents in the surrounding areas. Her willingness to take a strong stand in the face of status quo opposition has been evident nowhere more than in her 2025 vote against a multi-billion dollar expansion of the Los Angeles Convention Center, [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2025-09-19/city-council-approves-convention-center-amid-cost-concerns] which was backed both by the LA Fed and business interest groups, citing the massive expense to city funds.

Councilmember Raman’s relationship with DSA-LA, and the broader Angeleno grassroots left, has had some ups and downs. Raman is consistent in her support for renter protections and a humane, effective homelessness policy, but also has critiques of Measure ULA, the so-called “mansion tax,” which DSA-LA supported. She also began her political career with an inconsistent position on Palestine, which eventually resulted in conflict with the chapter over a 2024 endorsement from a pro-Israel Dem club. Since then, however, she did what we would want any Socialist in Office we critique to do: she worked with us to improve on this issue. She was one of only three councilmembers to sponsor a resolution for a permanent ceasefire [https://lapublicpress.org/2024/09/los-angeles-ceasefire-resolution-status/#:~:text=In%20June%2C%20three%20Los%20Angeles%20City%20Council,unhindered%20humanitarian%20aid%20medical%20care%20to%20Gaza], achieved through months of organizing by DSA-LA members and coalition partners.

In September, she attended a forum run by the extremely pro-Zionist Jewish Federation, and not only said Israel is committing a genocide, but also that BDS is a legitimate form of protest that she would protect as mayor—for which the audience members booed her. Karen Bass, however, went on that same stage and denied Israel’s genocide. The contrast is clear, and Nithya is presenting a left-wing vision unafraid of speaking truth.

This is also evident in the forces backing her. Since the primary when we initially analyzed this race, Nithya has assembled a dynamic and exciting coalition of left-labor stalwarts: her crusading for tenants brought in the Alliance of Californians for Community Empowerment (ACCE), her plan to build desperately-needed housing units brought in the Western States Carpenters, her advocacy for higher wages for workers and strong enforcement on illegal short-term rentals earned her the endorsement of UNITE HERE Local 11, and her support for militant organized labor and thoughtful policy solutions brought in UAW Region 6. Nithya is also endorsed by the Working Families Party, the Writer’s Guild of America West, LA Forward, the Engineers and Architects Association, the Committee of Interns and Residents, and many more. [https://www.nithyaforthecity.com/endorsements] And, of course, us.

Nithya’s announcement came relatively late in the game, after our primary endorsement process had concluded, and the chapter voted narrowly not to reopen our endorsement process. However, in the third phase of our electoral endorsements, the chapter engaged in spirited and thorough debate on whether or not to endorse Nithya. Ultimately, our members voted 65.9% in favor of endorsing her, out of an electorate of roughly 1427 voters.

And it’s clear why: Nithya’s campaign is presenting a left-wing vision of a better city for all, one that has actually gotten stronger and more ambitious post-primary. Nithya’s skill at policy is on full display in the detailed programs she is advancing, from a bold and cost-effective initiative to cut homelessness by half [https://drive.google.com/file/d/1VFzop4OD2o62OaAvHfcGbJEUMV5Qt36K/view], to her plan to create an Office of Tenant Protection [https://www.nithyaforthecity.com/renters] to keep renters in their homes, to her plans to fix Los Angeles’s deadly street infrastructure [https://www.nithyaforthecity.com/transportation] and expand our transit system. She advocated for taking on the powerful and ultra-reactionary Los Angeles Police Protective League, expanding unarmed crisis response, actually enforcing our sanctuary city ordinance (which she co-wrote) and protecting our city’s street vendors—who Karen Bass promised to sweep. All in all, this is a campaign that upholds democratic socialist values, and makes immediate change for working-class people in Los Angeles.

It’s clear that the city needs the kind of bold, thoughtful change that a democratic socialist mayor can bring, especially one who can focus on fundamentals in the tradition of the “sewer socialists” of old. Nithya Raman is running as a pothole populist, someone who knows and loves the city of Los Angeles and thinks it is time for that city to work for its people—not the coterie of neoliberal interests that prop up Karen Bass. And we’ll note: while no single LA politician can be “LA’s Zohran,” Mayor Nithya Raman, City Attorney Marissa Roy, and Councilmember Estuardo Mazariegos to grow the socialist bloc on City Council would give us a powerful foundation for real change in this city.

Democratic socialist Nithya Raman is running to fix the broken status quo of Los Angeles, and she has the knowledge and courage to do it.

DSA-LA enthusiastically endorses Nithya Raman for mayor because Angelenos deserve a city that works for them—not landlords and corporate interests.

### City Attorney


Marissa Roy

🌹 Endorsed

Marissa Roy is a lifelong Angeleno and civil rights attorney who has spent her career fighting for working people—families, renters, immigrants, and all Californians. She is the first citywide candidate that DSA-LA has ever endorsed, and the seat with the most individually-situated power that we have ever attempted to win.

Roy has dedicated her entire career as an attorney delivering civil rights and consumer protection litigation on behalf of local and California State governments. She's helped build landmark lawsuits against companies that exploited workers, taken on Trump in court, and even sued tech giants like Meta and won [https://www.npr.org/2026/08/26/nx-s1-5944781/meta-settlement-child-safety-lawsuit]. In previous roles, she helped build cases against large employers, like trucking companies at the Port of Los Angeles and Carwash companies who were exploiting their workers. Endorsers of her campaign include: SEIU 721, Teamsters Joint Council 42, NUHW, UTLA, ILWU Southern District Council, and the LA Fed. This level of support, particularly from organized labor, for a young, democratic socialist challenger is highly unusual, and speaks to the depth of her many years of work defending workers’ rights.

Roy has been an active DSA-LA member for years, representing our chapter at national conventions and co-leading an electoral working group in the 2024 electoral cycle. Likewise, she received strong support from our chapter through the endorsement process, with 93.8% of voting members approving her endorsement.

John McKinney, is a former district attorney prosecutor, running on a slightly conservative law-and-order “compassionate accountability” platform of prosecuting crime and improving transparency. He has made his bed with Republicans for this campaign: his campaign fundraisers are Pluvious [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2019-12-07/jackie-lacey-campaign-cuts-ties-with-fundraiser-who-previously-had-role-in], who has hosted an event for Donald Trump this year. He’s paid $259,800 to Strategy Insights, a firm run by Matt Rexroad, a Republican strategist described as “a key adviser to Republicans, including House Minority Leader, and Speaker-in-waiting, Kevin McCarthy.” AirBnB, Rick Caruso, and other ghoulish, capitalist interests have poured money into McKinney’s campaign. He has received endorsements from right-wing LA District Attorney Nathan Hochman and the Los Angeles Police Protective League (who revoked their endorsement of sitting City Attorney Hydee Feldstein Soto after her office allowed and then allegedly covered up a data breach [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-05-05/la-city-attorney-challenger-district-attorney-police-union]).

Marissa Roy is running to make sure that the powerful role of Los Angeles City Attorney protects those who need it most. She was the biggest vote-getter in the primary, and it is time to seal the deal.

Cast your vote for Marissa Roy this November.

### City Council District 3

BW

Barri Worth Girvan

👍 Recommended

Los Angeles CD 3 covers much of the West San Fernando Valley, including Woodland Hills, West Hills, Tarzana, Winnetka, and Reseda. Current councilmember Bob Blumenfeld is termed out, leaving us two potential candidates to replace him: Tim Gaspar and Barri Worth Girvan.

Gaspar is Blumenfield’s chosen successor. He touts endorsements from the Los Angeles Police Protective League and many of the moderate to conservative elected representatives in the SFV. According to his website, his priorities involve “restoring law and order in every neighborhood,” supporting and expanding 41.18 encampment bans, and cutting red tape for small businesses. A self-described "entrepreneur" and founder of Gaspar Insurance, Gaspar lists donations from real estate, the Apartment Association of Greater Los Angeles, and a bevy of other donors who donate to candidates who run against DSA-LA endorsees. Gaspar and the opponents to DSA-LA’s endorsed candidates for Council District 9 and City Attorney recently participated in the sparsely attended “Los Angeles Clean & Safe Forum,” [https://www.facebook.com/reel/1400513842280040/] hosted by the Los Angeles Hospitality Partnership [https://www.hospitalitypartnership.la/] at the Los Angeles Police Museum, a true Mad Libs nightmare of an event (even featuring AI-ified headshots of the candidates on the flyer—seriously, who are these guys?).

[img alt="Flyer for a community conversation featuring Tim Gaspar, Jose Ugarte and John McKinney at the Los Angeles Police Museum" src=/media/general/cd3-gaspar-flyer.webp]

Girvan has the most labor endorsements in the race, with the LA Federation of Labor endorsement along with SEIU 721, NUHW, AFT 1521, and more. She also lists a handful of endorsements from various eras of the LA political status quo, including her old boss, Lindsay Horvath, and many within the state assembly where she used to work. While no socialist, her platform includes references to properly expanding unarmed crisis response teams, humane solutions to homelessness, including centralizing homelessness outreach and support within the city, and ensuring housing is built quickly and by transit and jobs hubs.

While Girvan is no socialist, she would be a step forward in the SFV in the face of a candidate who wants to increase policing and criminalization of homelessness.

DSA-LA recommends Barri Worth Girvan for Council District 3.

### City Council District 9


Estuardo Mazariegos

🌹 Endorsed

CD 9 spans parts of Los Angeles south of Downtown LA, including South Central Los Angeles, Vermont Square, Exposition Park, and the neighborhoods surrounding the Coliseum–including the city’s major World Cup and Olympic venues. It is LA’s lowest-income city council district and one of the most heavily impacted by displacement, environmental racism, unsafe streets, and chronic public underinvestment. With billions of dollars in development and global mega-events flowing through the district, this race will determine whether that wealth continues to be extracted or is redirected into housing, union jobs, and public goods for working-class residents.

DSA-LA proudly endorses Estuardo Mazariegos for City Council District 9. Mazariegos is a longtime tenant organizer and democratic socialist, a Guatemalan immigrant who has lived in South Central from early childhood and has spent nearly two decades building working-class power in his neighborhood. A community leader, Estuardo is a co-director at the Alliance of Californians for Community Empowerment (ACCE) and leader in the tenants’ rights movement, where he helped to pass the Resident Protection Ordinance [https://planning.lacity.gov/odocument/2c923930-d78d-460d-9f95-ce65cdc144cd/Fact_Sheet_Resident_Protection_Ordinance_FD.pdf], guaranteeing the right of return and substantial relocation assistance for tenants displaced by development. Mazariegos was on the frontlines of strengthening the Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance (TAHO) and expanding enforcement tools against landlord abuse. He also played a key role in passing Measure ULA (the mansion tax), working with the United to House LA coalition and ensuring that revenue from the high-value real estate tax was directed to fund affordable housing and homelessness prevention. He organized alongside tourism workers against corporate giants like Delta to secure the Olympic Wage [https://www.smdp.com/living-wage-for-all-coalition-launches-in-santa-monica-with-push-for-dollar30-countywide-minimum/], ensuring that profits from global mega-events are shared with workers and the community instead of being used to further subsidize corporate profits. Mazarigos has supported expanding income assistance for households impacted by immigration raids [https://www.estuardo4la.com/immigrantrights] and has consistently connected housing stability to immigrant defense. His platform centers social [https://www.estuardo4la.com/housing] housing [https://www.estuardo4la.com/housing], strict enforcement of tenant protections, reinvestment of stadium and dealership revenue into community infrastructure, expansion of local hire pipelines into union jobs [https://www.estuardo4la.com/olympics], and full compliance with sanctuary policies. Mazariegos’ campaign refuses corporate PAC, developer, and police association money; it is thus accountable to the grassroots donors and organized working-class institutions.

His opponent, Jose Ugarte, is the hand-picked successor to the current councilmember, Curren Price, and has served as an aide within that office. Price was indicted in 2023 on embezzlement charges, and, at the time of this writing, currently faces up to 9 years in state prison [https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/curren-price-los-angeles-city-council-corruption-charges-district-attorney-hochman/3763370/]. Ugarte appears to be following in his boss’s footsteps, racking up his own ethics violation for failing to disclose $400,000 in income from his personal lobbying and consulting firm [https://2urbangirls.com/2025/10/city-council-candidate-to-pay-17500-penalty-for-failing-to-report-consulting-income/]. The entirety of this income was generated by donations from campaigns and PACs in support of establishment figures like Karen Bass, Jimmy Gomez, and Tim McOsker. He has raised significant funding from the same status quo coalition of developers, business interests, and institutional players that have dominated district politics for years. His campaign messaging relies heavily on identity-based appeals, while offering little structural analysis of displacement, landlord power, or wealth extraction from the district. On key issues, his framing leans toward law-and-order politics and market-driven development strategies that have defined the current era of governance in CD9. With establishment backing and institutional fundraising networks, he is treated by insiders as a presumptive, and equally corruptible, successor.

CD 9 cannot afford another term of extraction politics that treats South Central as a site for spectacle and profit while working-class residents bear the costs. The district contains world-class venues and some of the city’s deepest inequality, putting the nauseating inequality of Los Angeles in plain view.

This race offers a clear choice between a program rooted in social housing, labor power, and democratic accountability - or one that continues an untenable status quo.

Vote Estuardo Mazariegos for Los Angeles City Council District 9.

## Los Angeles County

### County Sheriff

AV

Alex Villanueva

Opposed

We’ve said it every voter guide: there are no good cops and even fewer good sheriffs.

But we’re going to give you our analysis anyway.

Incumbent Sheriff Robert Luna has institutional backing with endorsements from all 5 LA County Supervisors, Mayor Karen Bass, Senator Alex Padilla, and other state and local elected officials, as well as LA County Federation of Labor. His campaign is running on a couple major headlines: violent crime falling every year during his term and homicides down 25% since 2023. He’s responsible for increased law enforcement on the Metro system and instituting body-worn cameras in jails. In 2024, he announced the creation of the office of constitutional policing as part of his policy to formally ban deputy gangs, which he ran on in 2022. Critics question whether this has been successful.

### Supreme Court & Appellate Court Justices

Did you think choosing between two candidates for judges with extremely little info about them was too simple? Do you want an even more confusing, even lower-information judicial election? Congratulations, here’s your chance!

Voters in Los Angeles have an opportunity to choose whether to retain Supreme Court and Appellate court justices who have been appointed to their positions. These votes come up every six or twelve years, and approve or remove judges who have previously been appointed by a governor and approved by the “Commission on Judicial Appointments”, which is made up of the state’s Chief Justice, its Attorney General, and a senior authority from the local appellate court.

Appellate court justices form three-judge panels to review decisions made by lower courts within a particular district, to ensure that decisions were fair. They can choose to reject appeals and not review cases, and don’t consider traffic infractions, civil cases under $35000, or misdemeanors. The appellate court for Los Angeles County also covers San Luis Obispo and Santa Barbara.

Decisions of the appellate courts can be appealed up to the state Supreme Court. Additionally, death penalty decisions are reviewed by the Supreme Court, although California has had no executions since 2006, Governor Newsom issued a moratorium on executions in 2019, and is reportedly considering commuting all previously-issued death sentences (of which there are 564).

No appeals court justice has been removed by a retention election since 1930. In 1986, three liberal California justices were removed after a ten million dollar campaign against them for consistently overturning death penalty decisions, allowing Republican Governor Deukmejian to create a conservative majority on the court. This year, there’s no such campaign, and no reason to believe any of these justices will not be retained.

You would honestly be perfectly justified in voting yes on all of these judges. Or no. Or leaving it blank. Really this is a democratic backstop in case of a major court coup, and California’s appeals and supreme court systems have mostly been okay. Nearly every judge on your ballot was appointed by either Jerry Brown or Gavin Newsom, and a replacement by Newsom if one is not retained is unlikely to create any systemic change.

Nevertheless, here’s our brief rundown of each justice on the ballot.

Supreme Court

Joshua Groban was appointed by Jerry Brown in late 2018. Before appointment, Groban worked at different white-shoe law firms specializing in technology, healthcare, and energy, before advising on Brown’s 2010 gubernatorial campaign. As a corporate lawyer, he’s probably very technically skilled, but if you’re looking for a reason to vote no, that’s as good as any.

Kelli Evans worked for ACLU for Northern California before being appointed in 2022 by Gavin Newsom. With an interest and background in civil liberties, you should vote yes to retain her.

Appellate Court

Gregory J. Weingart, Steve Goorvitch, Brian M. Hoffstadt, and John Wiley were all federal prosecutors prior to their appointments. We generally oppose appointing prosecutors to judicial positions since they tend to emphasize punishment and incarceration. You could vote no on these four for that reason, although we’d be remiss if we didn’t point out that federal prosecution also includes fraud and white collar crime, and that Newsom would likely appoint prosecutors to replace them.

Audra M. Mori, Nicholas Daum, Natalie Stone, Tari Cody, Matthew A. Scherb, and Armen Tamzarian were all partners in corporate law, with specializations in media and entertainment litigation, copyright and trademarks, and for Tamzarian, business and real estate. You could vote no on these six for that reason, although we should also keep in mind that having a wide variety of expertise on the appellate court is important for its ability to adjudicate higher-level cases.

### City of Inglewood Measure MM

Vote YES

OK, this last one isn’t related to the stadiums. Inglewood voters are being asked to approve $396 million in bonds to modernize schools throughout the city’s school district. This measure would add to the more than $450 million previously approved school modernization bonds over the last 30 years, just as the district looks likely to exit 14 years of state receivership. Under receivership, the state has essentially administered the district in exchange for a $55 million emergency loan to address the district’s fiscal insolvency. The district’s structural deficit has several causes, notably a decrease in the school-age population (intensified by gentrification and displacement) in a state that ties funding to daily attendance numbers, and the extreme impact of charter schools in Inglewood—fully one-third of Inglewood students have been siphoned out of the district into charters, creating a devastating fiscal crisis for the district’s remaining students, and Inglewood’s teachers remain among the lowest-paid teachers in Southern California.

Measure MM addresses none of these massive structural problems. This is why the Inglewood Teachers Association opposed Measure MM, with President John Hughes writing in an op-ed, “Teachers understand better than most that our schools need investment. We work in these buildings every day.... Bond funds cannot solve the ongoing problem of educator compensation.” Bonds are paid off by property owners, and much of the rest of the opposition to the measure takes a Howard Jarvis-like tone, bemoaning another new tax. While most individual homeowners will likely pay a couple hundred dollars a year for these bonds, the measure will bring in a ton of revenue from billionaires like Stan Kroenke and Steve Ballmer, whose SoFi Stadium and Intuit Dome are cornerstones of Inglewood’s property tax revenues—Proposition 13 makes this nearly impossible to calculate, but Kroenke alone pays probably more than 10% of Inglewood’s overall property tax revenues, for properties making up fully 25% of the assessed property value in the city.

As always, though, bonds are not a good socialist method of generating public revenue. By issuing bonds to be traded on the public market, they institutionalize debt through financialization. From a socialist perspective, the ideal solution would be to fund public education through progressive taxation of wealth and high incomes, while investing those resources into infrastructure that remains publicly owned and available as a public good. Measure MM is not that. It may provide meaningful resources but it also reinforces finance capital, and wouldn’t pass muster for a DSA campaign endorsement.

The teachers’ union is right that bond measures like MM don’t pay for teacher salaries, support staff, mental health and family services, or most of the other things kids need to learn. They do pay for new buildings, which students need even in the face of declining enrollment, especially as the district has been forced to compete with charters for students.

### County of LA Measure A

Vote NO

Measure A is a County Charter amendment covering 14 public safety bargaining units. The covered workers include firefighters, deputy sheriffs, DA investigators, deputy medical examiners, lifeguards, their supervisors, and nonadministrative civilian workers in the Fire, Sheriff’s, and Medical Examiner departments. It does four main things:

- Bans strikes. Any covered worker who willfully joins an illegal strike or work stoppage could face discipline up to and including firing. This sounds really bad but actually most of the units covered are already banned from striking as public safety workers. But it adds two new building trades units and bans them from striking. So it is bad, just a little less bad than it sounds.

- Replaces bargaining deadlocks with binding arbitration. When contract renewal talks stall, a three-member panel decides. The County and the union each appoint one member, and the two sides select a neutral chair. The panel picks one side’s final offer on each issue.

- Makes awards binding without a vote. Once an award is final, “no other actions by the Board of Supervisors or by the electorate” to confirm or approve it are permitted.

- Freezes current terms. Existing wages and conditions stay in place until a deal or award is reached, so the County cannot impose a contract.

Measure A would affect covered workers differently. For sworn sheriff deputies and firefighters, who are already prohibited from striking, binding arbitration would provide a new tool when negotiations reach impasse. Rather than allowing the county to ultimately impose its final offer, unresolved issues would go to a neutral arbitration panel. If you’re already banned from striking, that can help workers get better contracts than they would otherwise.

But this measure was not built around lifeguards. The deputy sheriffs' union, ALADS, is its largest funder at more than $2 million, and it represents more than 8,000 deputies. ALADS has been without a salary contract since January 2025 and is bargaining with the County right now. The board subsequently voted to place its own measure on the November ballot.

That creates broader fiscal concerns. County officials have warned that arbitration awards not tied to the county’s budget policies or financial projections could strain reserves and limit its ability to fund other priorities particularly as the county faces increased fiscal pressures from federal healthcare cuts, wildfire recovery, and major legal settlements. When combined with the fact that the relevant union currently negotiating with the county is that for County Sheriffs– a notoriously corrupt “law enforcement” agency– it looks even worse.

Further, for covered civilian and non-sworn officers who currently retain a legal right to strike, the measure would take away that right. Binding arbitration can sometimes help workers, but as socialists we should always prioritize the right and power that comes from organizing collectively as workers to strike.

Vote No.

### City of LA Proposition TE

Vote NO

Proposition TE carves out an exemption from Measure ULA, which funds affordable housing and tenant support. The exemption covers residential properties, including apartment buildings, damaged or destroyed in the Palisades Fire. Any qualifying sale between January 7, 2025, and January 6, 2030, by whoever owned the property the day the fire started, gets a one-time exemption or refund. Refunds reach back to ULA taxes already paid.

ULA applies only to sales above an inflation-adjusted threshold. The threshold is currently $5.4 million for the 4% rate and $10.9 million for the 5.5% rate. Anyone TE helps is, by definition, selling a property worth more than $5.4 million. TE does not ask whether the property was the seller’s only home, whether they lived there, or whether they are struggling. It asks only whether the property was damaged and whether the seller owned it on January 7, 2025.

The City Administrative Officer estimates TE will cost the House LA Fund $35 million to $66 million through January 2030. The estimate is based partly on rebuilt Palisades homes already listed for sale, which means some of the benefit goes to owners who rebuilt and then sold, not only to people forced to sell burned lots. The House LA Fund pays for affordable housing production and homelessness prevention, including legal help for tenants facing eviction. The City’s Chief Legislative Analyst says TE “may lead to... a decrease of funding for affordable housing and tenant assistance programs”. In other words, the fund that pays for eviction defense and affordable housing would give up revenue so that sellers of $5.4 million-plus properties can skip the tax.

TE is backed by Mayor Karen Bass, Councilmember Traci Park, council President Marqueece Harris-Dawson, the Western States Regional Council of Carpenters, and Palisades Community Council; no formal opposition was submitted.

Fire survivors deserve to be made whole, particularly the renters displaced by the fire, for whom TE does nothing. Because of the political optics of the Palisades fires, this is almost certain to pass, but we should understand this initiative's economic impacts for what they are: an initiative to cut public funding that helps low-income Angelenos in order to lower taxes for the wealthiest homeowners affected by the fires.

Vote NO.

### City of LA Charter Amendment LA

Vote YES

Charter Amendment LA is a package of reforms aimed at modernizing how LA manages its money, infrastructure and public services. It would move the City toward two-year budgeting, require long-term infrastructure planning, reform the Department of Public Works,and update contracting rules.

Particularly exciting, LA's Charter currently bars the City from running purely commercial or industrial businesses unless voters approve each one at the ballot box. Amendment LA removes that requirement, clearing the way for future publicly owned alternatives to private businesses and services.

There are minor concerns about other provisions, particularly expanded authority to mortgage City property and changes to the Board of Public Works. But the amendment also gives LA tools for stronger long-term public investment and municipal ownership. Two-year budgeting cycles will also give our city government more ability to intentionally fund longer-term infrastructure projects, instead of the annual race to cover budget holes caused by LAPD overspending in order to generate better headlines [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-04-29/la-considers-expanding-airbnb-style-short-term-vacation-rentals].

City Controller Kenneth Mejia and Councilmembers Bob Blumenfield and Katy Yaroslavsky authored the official argument in support, and the Council, including our four socialist councilmembers in office, voted 13–0 to put the measure before voters.

For us, removing restrictions on public enterprise is a major opportunity to expand what our city can directly provide for Angelenos, so vote YES on charter amendment LA.

### City of LA Charter Amendment PL

Vote YES

LA has an infamously labyrinthian planning system. We wish it was the good kind of labyrinthian, with David Bowie and Jim Henson creations. Instead, it’s the bad kind of labyrinthian (confusing).

What it does

Charter Amendment PL tries to simplify things in three ways:

- One appeals body instead of seven. The City's seven Area Planning Commissions, with 35 commissioners between them, would be replaced by a single citywide Neighborhood Appeals Commission of at least seven members.

- More authority for City Planning. The Planning Department would get clearer power to decide certain land-use cases itself.

- No more death by inaction. Today, if a General Plan amendment has been approved by both the City Planning Commission and the Mayor and the Council doesn't act within 75 days, it is denied. Under PL, it would be approved. Supporters say the City Council should have to kill something with a vote, not by running out the clock. Former DTLA Councilmember Huizar’s infamous corruption case involved his failing to agendize plan items until their sponsors bribed him.

LA’s planning process can be painfully slow and confusing, and reducing unnecessary bureaucracy isn’t inherently bad. Area Planning Commissions matter far less than they used to. State and local changes have made many project approvals ministerial, so there is less for them to hear. The seven commissions currently have 35 commissioners in total; Measure PL would replace them with a single commission of at least 7 members. [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-10-01/2026-california-election-los-angeles-city-charter-amendment-la-voter-guide] If passed, this would make small but noticeable reforms to approve new construction with fewer opportunities for municipal corruption.

The measure is supported by Charter Reform Commission chair Raymond Meza and Roy Samaan, President of the Engineers & Architects Association; no formal opposition was submitted.

Ultimately, this amendment is legitimately mostly streamlining an outdated planning process.

Vote yes on PL.

### City of LA Charter Amendment EE

No recommendation

Charter Amendment EE combines several changes to City ethics, oversight, elections, and City Council operations On the positive side, it strengthens independent oversight bodies for LAPD and the Fire Department by allowing them to conduct audits, increases penalties for campaign finance violations, and creates a five year waiting period before ethics commissioners or the ethics commission executive director can run for city Council or LAUSD School Board Considering City Hall’s tenuous relationship with ethics over the years, we’re certainly not going to complain about stronger accountability.

EE also changes the referendum process. Currently, Angelenos seeking to overturn a City Council Action must collect signatures equal to 10% of the votes cast in the most recent mayoral election; EE would raise that to 15%, the same threshold required for a voter initiative. Supporters argue that changing a law and overturning one should require the same level of demonstrated public support. And referendum power is not inherently progressive: the most recent attempt was backed by major business interests seeking to overturn the City’s $30 minimum wage for tourism workers. That campaign, backed by airlines and hotel interests, still fell about 9,000 signatures short under the current rules.

On the other hand, referendum campaigns have only 30 days to gather signatures, compared with four months for initiative. Raising the threshold from roughly 93,000 signatures under current turnout levels to nearly 140,000 could therefore make referendums impossible for grassroots movements. On the other hand, they effectively already are impossible if you aren’t able to fund a multi-million dollar campaign.

EE also lowers the minimum number of required City Council meetings from three days a week to once a week. Council “public comment” watchdogs claim this would mean city council would spend less time “doing their jobs” and listening to the public. On the other hand, anybody who’s been to public comment knows that city council isn’t listening anyway and half the time there’s nothing valuable to listen to [https://abc7.com/post/man-arrested-for-criminal-threats-hate-speech-at-la-city-council-meeting/1345395/].

Ultimately, this is mostly a technical set of changes, some very good, some somewhat bad.

We’re not making a recommendation.

### City of LA Charter Amendment PRK

Vote YES

Charter Amendment PRK is about LA’s parks needing money. (Get it, PaRK)

The measure would gradually double the minimum funding the City is required to provide its Department of Recreation and Parks over the next 10 years. It does not create a new tax or source of revenue; instead, it increases the share of existing City revenues guaranteed to parks under the charter. Currently, the charter requires the department to receive.0325% of the total assessed value of taxable properties in the City. Under the existing formula, that minimum is projected to grow to $303 million by 2036, PRK would instead increase it to roughly $995 million [https://cityclerk.lacity.org/election/Measure_PRK.pdf].

And they could use it. LA’s parks ranked 93rd among the nation’s 100 largest cities in the Trust for Public Land’s 2026 ParkScore and the City’s own park assessment found that LA spends about two-thirds less on parks than comparable cities [https://cityclerk.lacity.org/election/Measure_PRK.pdf]. LA has more than 500 parks, 150+ recreation and senior centers and 59 pools, while the department has struggled with staffing shortages, aging facilities and a massive maintenance backlog. More guaranteed funding means more money for things like playground and bathroom repairs, park cleaning, and money for the workers keeping these places running. The [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-10-01/2026-election-los-angeles-city-charter-amendment-prk-voter-guide] Los Angeles Police Protective League opposes the measure, [https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-10-01/2026-election-los-angeles-city-charter-amendment-prk-voter-guide] arguing that guaranteed additional spending through the charter would leave the city with less flexibility to respond to future budget crises. Very thoughtful of them. Now, to take a big sip of coffee and look at how much money the city pays to bail out corrupt or abusive cops every year.

Because PRK does not create new revenue, however, the debate is ultimately over how the City prioritizes its existing resources including funding for parks relative to other departments. This amendment also has support from LAUSD’s Board of Education, who has noted that additional park funding could help expand Community School Parks. During a de [https://cityclerk.lacity.org/election/Measure_PRK.pdf]clared fiscal emergency, [https://cityclerk.lacity.org/election/Measure_PRK.pdf] the City Council could reduce the increased funding required by this measure by up to 30% for the duration of the emergency.

Public parks are a basic public good that every Angeleno should be able to enjoy, and LA has underfunded ours for far too long. It’s unfortunate and structurally poor governance that the method by which we’re funding them is a charter reform for guaranteed funding - it would be far better if our elected councilmembers had the political courage and principles to prioritize them instead of giving LAPD a 20th helicopter.

Vote YES on Charter Amendment PRK.

### City of LA Charter Amendment PRT

Vote YES

Charter Amendment PRT would update how the City runs its airports, its harbor, and the Department of Water and Power. (Get it: PoRT). It would:

- Increase the Airport Commission seats reserved for residents near LAX from one to at least three, and near Van Nuys from one to at least two, for a total of at least 5 local seats instead of two.

- Require the Harbor Department to devote a portion of its annual operating income to waterfront public access projects.

- Require applicants for certain Port leases or projects needing coastal development permits to disclose how the project would affect jobs.

- Allow the Council to extend airport and LADWP leases beyond the 30-year default to as long as 66 years. Such an extension would require a finding that the longer term serves the City and a two-thirds vote of the Council. The Mayor may veto the extension, and an override requires a three-fourths vote of the Council. The Council can already approve 50-year leases for the airport and LADWP, so the change adds up to 16 more years, but the longer term requires a two-thirds vote.

For residents of neighborhoods next to LAX and Van Nuys, who live with the noise and pollution, PRT would expand their guaranteed seats on the Airport Commission from two to five. Commissioners are still appointed through the usual process, so residency alone will not guarantee that they speak for affected communities.

PRT would also require applicants for certain Port leases or coastal permits to report the expected effects on employment and the regional economy before the Port acts. Applicants would pay for these disclosures themselves. That would put job impacts on the public record, giving dockworkers, other Port workers, and the community something concrete to organize around.

However, the waterfront money would not be guaranteed at current levels. The City says PRT “codifies” the Port’s practice of putting 10% of operating income into public access projects, roughly $28.6 million a year. The Charter text requires only “a portion,” and it gives the Harbor Commission “the sole authority to determine the appropriate amount” each year. Nothing would stop the commission from going below 10%. Today, though, the Charter requires no waterfront investment at all, so PRT at least sets a floor above zero.

Even so, we believe PRT is a net gain for communities near the airports and for Port workers. It’s still mostly a technical update, and we should keep watching and organizing towards better public ownership.

Vote YES on PRT.

### City of LA Charter Amendment SC

No recommendation

Charter Amendment SC is mostly housekeeping. The City Charter currently has two long, nearly identical sets of campaign finance rules: one for LAUSD Board of Education candidates and one for City candidates. SC deletes the school board version and says board candidates follow the same rules as candidates for Mayor. The following rules would not cover school board candidates:

- Limits on fundraising from City contractors and lobbyists.

- Access to public matching funds.

These are rules that already don’t apply to LAUSD races today, but changing them in the future would require another future charter amendment. That’s timely, because a state Proposition 4 this year will make it legal for more races to use public financing rules, and there’s a chance that new movements for public election financing come for LAUSD.

Two things are changing:

- LA City Council. Today, changing the school board's campaign rules takes a public votes. Under SC, the City Council could add restrictions or carve out exceptions by ordinance. That includes details the Charter currently spells out, like the rules on outside spending coordinated with a candidate and the 24-hour notice for independent expenditures of $1,000 or more. The Council could use that power well or badly.

- Fewer people can sue. Today, any resident of the school district can go to court over most violations. Under the mayoral rules, only City residents and the City Attorney can. LAUSD residents in cities like Huntington Park, South Gate and West Hollywood would lose that right. Private plaintiffs would also have to go to the City Attorney first, not the Ethics Commission.

One smaller wrinkle: a catch-all court penalty would drop from $5,000 to $2,000 per violation, unless Measure EE passes, in which case the cap would raise to $15,000. See our recommendation on Charter Amendment EE.

SC tidies up the Charter and takes a little power from voters and non-City residents along the way. That isn't enough to oppose it, nor enough to cheer for it.

No recommendation on Charter Amendment SC.

### City of LA Measure FD

No recommendation

Measure FD proposes a half-cent sales tax increase in the City of Los Angeles, raising the total rate from 10.25% to 10.75%. The City estimates FD would raise about $345 million a year, all in a separate fund for the Los Angeles Fire Department.

The money goes into a separate fund for firefighters and paramedics, engines and ambulances, fire stations, and brush clearance. Supporters say it could pay for at least 1,000 new firefighter and paramedic positions, and it would fund new stations, built under a union Project Labor Agreement at prevailing wages.

Measure FD attempts to prevent the City from replacing existing LAFD funding with the new revenue through a Maintenance of Effort rule. It locks in LAFD’s average share of the City’s discretionary budget over the 10 years before the tax starts as a minimum percentage that cannot be adjusted downward. If the adopted budget falls short of that share, the City loses the right to collect the tax that year.

There is no question that LAFD needs greater investment. The department has significantly fewer firefighters per capita (about 0.9 per thousand residents) than other major American cities, which generally range from 1.2-1.8 per thousand. And the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires show starkly that as climate change accelerates, the city will need to invest in far better wildfire preparedness. That’s why this measure has political support from across the ideological spectrum. It’s probably going to pass.

The only registered argument against Measure FD is that the City should fund LAFD from its existing $8 billion-plus General Fund rather than a new tax. We agree in principle. However, the only organized opposition making that argument is the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, who propose paying for LAFD by cutting homelessness services and are generally among the Worst Organizations In the United States. We reject the premise that Angelenos should have to choose between adequately funding emergency services and providing services to unhoused residents.

But we’d be remiss if we didn’t register our concerns. Sales taxes are regressive, requiring lower income households to pay a larger share compared to wealthier households. ### Proposition 1

Vote YES

Prop 1 authorizes $11.25 billion in state bonds, mainly for multifamily affordable housing, preservation/supportive housing, veterans' housing, homeownership assistance, and farmworker/tribal/student housing.

California's housing crisis is fundamentally a result of housing being treated as a commodity; working-class tenants bear the consequences through enormous rents, displacement, and homelessness. Prop 1 is a flawed solution, but the positives outweigh the negatives. These bonds create a real public subsidy for housing that the private market will not produce at affordable rents. The proposition includes billions for affordable rental construction/preservation and supportive housing. However, as socialists we must note that bonds are a bad way to finance public investment compared with progressive taxation: the state ultimately pays hundreds of millions annually in debt service to bondholders. This is fundamentally a band-aid; we need to tax the rich and build state capacity to build social housing at scale, on the road to decommodifying housing.

Prop 1 is supported by some developer and real estate interests, but also by most affordable housing advocates and organized labor. We recommend a Yes vote.

### Proposition 2

Vote YES

Prop 2 raises the state reserve cap from 10% to 20%, requires more saving when revenues surge, and importantly excludes deposits into those reserves from California's Gann spending limit.

Recessions routinely become excuses for austerity. Capitalists use economic precarity to justify cuts to schools, health care, public workers, and social programs precisely when working people need them most. Larger reserves give the state greater ability to maintain services during downturns, rather than balancing budgets on workers and service recipients. Excluding reserve deposits from the Gann Limit also slightly weakens one of California's artificial constitutional constraints on public spending.

The California Labor Federation has endorsed Prop 2, as have most progressive institutional endorsers. Opposition comes primarily from Republicans and fiscal conservatives who are hostile to redistributive politics.

Saving money rather than spending it immediately is not intrinsically progressive; socialists generally don't fetishize balanced budgets or reserves for their own sake. However, this proposal leaves workers better off in the long run by providing more security at the state level. A larger cushion against austerity is better than mandated tax rebates/cuts triggered by California's fiscal constitution.

We recommend a Yes vote.

### Proposition 4

Vote YES

🌹 Endorsed by California DSA

Prop 4 repeals California's ban on state/local public campaign-finance systems and allows governments to establish programs for qualifying candidates who accept spending limits. It doesn't itself create a statewide public-financing program.

Electoral politics systematically privileges people who can raise money from wealthy donors, corporations, and professional political networks. In cities and states across the country, public financing of elections has allowed socialists and working people to compete on something at least marginally closer to a level playing field. Many socialist wins across the country, like Zohran Mamdani in New York City, would not have been possible without public financing. This is a clear positive step for working people, and can reduce candidates' dependence on wealthy private donors and allow working class mass membership organizations like our own to participate in larger and more expensive elections.

Prop 4 isn’t a silver bullet: it’s only enabling legislation, not the final reform. Winning Prop 4 creates space for socialists and reformers to fight locally/statewide over what strong public-finance systems actually look like. Public financing also does not eliminate capitalist political power, independent expenditures, lobbying, or wealthy ownership of media, or even come close to leveling the playing field. It is, however, an important and necessary step forward for working class political power.

California DSA has endorsed a Yes vote.

### Proposition 5

Vote YES

Prop 5 ends the simultaneous successor contest when a statewide officer is recalled. If the governor is removed, the lieutenant governor succeeds; other vacancies are filled under normal law rather than by a second question on the recall ballot.

This is not a sexy reform, nor is it a socialist one, but it is basic good government housekeeping. California's recall system creates a bizarre democratic loophole in which a majority can elect someone normally, then a smaller and differently composed electorate can remove them and potentially install a replacement with only a small plurality. Previous gubernatorial recalls have had dozens or even hundreds of candidates on the ballot, and often only recall supporters will even vote in the simultaneous successor contest, meaning a tiny plurality of voters can choose the governor.

The recall mechanism should answer the democratic question “should this official be removed?” rather than create an alternative low-turnout route to power for a minority of voters. Despite its abuse in California, socialists shouldn't oppose recall as a democratic mechanism itself. Recall has long been a democratic demand of historical socialist movements. We should, however, eliminate the anti-majoritarian successor-election mechanism.

We recommend a Yes vote.

### Proposition 45

Vote NO

Prop 45 is an initiative statute backed by the California Chamber of Commerce that makes sweeping changes to the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). Prop 45 creates a fast-track version of CEQA for a broad range of “essential” projects, including housing, transportation, energy, water, healthcare, schools, broadband, and public-safety infrastructure.

CEQA can be and is regularly abused to delay housing, transit, clean energy, and other necessary infrastructure at the expense of working class Californians. We don’t default to defending all regulatory and permitting processes just because they give the government power over private business. This is a genuinely thorny issue, with many costs and benefits and bad actors on both sides that can be difficult for socialists to disentangle. CEQA does desperately need further reform. Prop 45 sets fast timelines. It also limits agencies' obligation to study feasible alternatives and restricts the evidence and remedies available to courts reviewing approvals for a broad range of housing and infrastructure projects. Beyond this, it gives much of the housing it fast-tracks weaker labor standards than the other “essential” projects it covers.

For socialists, the key question we care about here is who gains power when review is weakened? As certified Bad Guy Alec Baldwin says in The Departed, “cui bono?” A good reform would accelerate democratically planned affordable housing, transit, and green infrastructure, while preserving workers' and affected communities' power over pollution and development. Prop 45 instead applies broad deregulation across many kinds of private and public projects, concentrating this power in the hands of developers themselves. There’s a reason for this. This reform was written by and for some of the most rapacious capitalists in the state.

The Yes campaign is heavily financed by the Building Industry Association, Chamber of Commerce, Edison, Realtors, developers, and billionaire cutout Building a Better California, a veritable rogues gallery of California politics. The No campaign includes most environmental groups and the state building trades. The latter is notable: the building trades will generally support nearly all construction. The old joke is they would pave over the Pacific Ocean if there was a project labor agreement.

California has already undertaken CEQA reform via AB 130 and SB 131, which broadly exempt urban infill housing from CEQA review. Prop 45’s new reforms use the word “essential” to bring in drastic new changes: covering not only apartments and transit but highways and road expansions, subdivisions, private medical-office buildings, major water systems, power infrastructure, and more. Expanding urban housing and giant infrastructure projects present radically different environmental problems, and need different regulatory regimes. We need more housing and transit; that doesn’t mean gutting regulation on highways and data centers. CEQA has been abused by wealthy homeowners, yes, but it has also been a vital tool for working class communities fighting warehouses, freeway expansions, industrial pollution, contaminated sites, and infrastructure that capitalists and more politically powerful actors want to dump in their communities. The answer is to make it harder for wealthy NIMBYs to block socially useful housing and transit, not to strip the ability of under-resourced working class Californians to fight environmental harm.

If passed, this proposition can only be legislatively amended at all by a 2/3rds vote of the legislature. Any defects or unintended consequences would therefore be incredibly difficult to fix through the ordinary democratic process. California history is littered with examples of poorly worded propositions creating seemingly permanent unintended negative consequences.

CEQA needs reform; California needs a faster process to allow our state to build housing at the needed scale. The housing crunch has been incredibly damaging to working class Californians and their ability to live in this state. CEQA needing further reform doesn’t mean developers and the Chamber of Commerce should be writing that reform. Socialists should support targeted reform, not less reform. This means by-right approval for affordable/social housing, transit, and clean energy. We should support greater public planning capacity; strong labor standards; and targeted limits on bad-faith litigation from wealthy and well-connected homeowners who want to block affordable housing and public transit. But we still need community and environmental protections, as well as a democratic process for development. Prop 45 is not the solution.

Thanks, California DSA! Now, back to the rest of our voter guide.

We recommend a No vote.
