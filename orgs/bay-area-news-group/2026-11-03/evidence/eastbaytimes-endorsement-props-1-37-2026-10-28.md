PUBLISHED: October 26, 2026 at 12:01 PM PDT | UPDATED: October 26, 2026 at 3:13 PM PDT

The problem underlying California’s housing crisis comes down to this: the cost of construction.

It constrains new supply. And the only way to recoup the costs of what does get built requires high rents and mortgages or — in the case of affordable housing — public subsidies.

Unfortunately, the solution to this crisis offered by two statewide propositions has nothing to do with addressing prohibitive building costs. Instead, their answer is to load Californians with debt while delivering bonanzas to home developers and builders.

Proposition 1 leaves taxpayers indebted; Proposition 37, homebuyers.

Reject both.

#### Proposition 1

This measure, placed on the ballot by the state Legislature through Senate Bill 407, is an expensive pandering potpourri of giveaways. Veterans, farmworkers, students, tribes are all the ostensible beneficiaries.

Here’s how it is supposed to work:

Proposition 1 would authorize the state to sell $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds, all earmarked for various housing programs. The bulk of the bonds, $7.2 billion, would be spent acquiring, building or renovating apartments. The backers of this proposition optimistically estimate that it would subsidize the construction of “up to” 40,000 multifamily rental units as well as 2,500 housing units for farmworkers and 1,200 beds for university students.

Another $1.1 billion would provide homeownership assistance for 40,000 families. The remainder of funds would go toward infrastructure projects ($500 million), farmworker housing ($450 million), student housing at the UC and Cal State systems ($350 million), and tribal housing ($200 million).

Additionally, $1.25 billion would be dedicated to home loan assistance for veterans. (This program is not expected to create direct costs as participating veterans would pay monthly payments to the state, according to the Legislative Analyst’s Office.)

The need for affordable housing and housing assistance in this state is undeniable. More than two in five households spend, at least, 30% of their incomes on housing.

However, Proposition 1 would only provide a drop in the bucket towards adding to the state’s housing supply, all while hamstringing state spending and burdening taxpayers with decades of debt. The LAO projects that paying off $10 billion in bond debt incurred by this measure would require annual payments between $500 million to $600 million for a quarter of a century. These payments would come from California’s checking account, the General Fund. Because the state has to pay interest on the money it borrows, per the LAO, the total cost of the bond would be about 15% more (after adjusting for inflation) than if the state paid up front with money it already has.

Perhaps that would be acceptable if this burst of spending would make a long-term difference, but it’s doubtful.

Consider that California needs to build 2.5 million new homes, including 1 million affordable ones, to keep up with demand.

But this measure would add only 4% toward the affordable total, and that’s only under pie-in-the-sky circumstances.

More disconcerting is how this measure would do nothing to attack the root of the affordable housing cost problem:

Building subsidized housing in California costs 1.5 times the average cost of building already-exorbitant market-rate housing here, according to a 2025 RAND study.

The nonpartisan think tank identified two major reasons for the discrepancy: Local governments charge “unusually large” architectural and engineering fees, and they require that affordable housing developers pay “substantially above-market” wages.

While $200 million of Proposition 1 would go toward “pilot” programs aimed at developing reduced housing models, at least $10 billion would be spent keeping this bloated model afloat.

#### Proposition 37

This measure looks great on the surface. It would help Californians buy new homes, all without costing taxpayers a dime.

Well, all that glitters isn’t gold.

Proposition 37 would authorize up to $25 billion in revenue bonds for downpayment assistance on newly constructed homes. Eligible buyers could borrow up to 17% of a home’s purchase price through a fixed-rate second mortgage, making a down payment as low as 3%.

For example, consider an $800,000 home. A buyer could put down $24,000, borrow $136,000 through Proposition 37 and then finance the remaining $640,000 using a conventional mortgage.

A 30-year loan at today’s rates would make the monthly costs of that second mortgage more than $900.

Proposition 37 is not reducing the burden of home prices in California.

It’s not solving the affordability crisis.

It’s just a creative way to help Californians take on more debt.

What rightly worries critics is the terms of these additional loans. When borrowers default, the primary mortgage lender gets repaid first, meaning that the owner of the secondary takes on more risk. That would almost certainly mean that investors would require that buyers pay higher rates on their second mortgages than they pay on the first.

The LAO identifies these financing terms and investor participation as significant uncertainties.

Proposition 37 does nothing to address the main obstacle to homeownership in the state: the monthly cost of a conventional mortgage.

While the majority of renters in the state have incomes that would make them eligible to benefit from the measure’s secondary mortgage program, many wouldn’t qualify for a conventional mortgage in the first place even with its down payment assistance, according to the Public Policy Institute of California.

Far from solving the problem of high home prices, Proposition 37 could compound it.

While the measure restricts using the downpayment assistance to previously unpurchased homes, that does not mean that Proposition 37 will unleash a large new supply of housing.

What’s infinitely more probable is that Proposition 37 would expand purchasing power faster than it would induce new construction — and therefore push up prices.

Homes in California, especially new ones, are sitting unsold longer. In fact, builders have created a glut of new housing in the Central Valley they can’t sell, a dynamic exacerbated as interest rates have climbed. In fact, this summer Fresno topped Realtor.com’s list of U.S. cities with the highest share of new home listings (68%) cutting prices. Builders in the region, like Lennar, are reportedly panicking and responding by offering steeper discounts to lure buyers.

But Proposition 37 would give future builders an escape hatch, if not a bonanza. Instead of having to lower prices, it could artificially inflate the market by putting (borrowed) cash into buyers’ hands.

No wonder realtors, carpenters and builders are throwing money to get this passed.

There is a role for government to play in California’s housing crisis. But these two measures aren’t it.

We can’t solve our way out by pumping more money into a broken system and calling creative ways to finance more debt the solution.

Reject Propositions 1 and 37.
