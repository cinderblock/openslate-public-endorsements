Statewide offices

Lieutenant governor — Fiona Ma

Success as this state’s No. 2 requires understanding its levers of power. Currently treasurer, Fiona Ma already knows how to leverage them. The next lieutenant governor must be as capable of navigating uncharted waters as the governor whom she or he might need to replace at a moment’s notice. By that standard, Fiona Ma is eminently qualified.

(Our interviews with the two candidates for lieutenant governor — Fiona Ma and Gloria Romero — are here.)

Treasurer — Eleni Kounalakis

California’s next treasurer should expect to inherit the most financially volatile environment since the Great Recession. The state faces long-running structural shortfalls. Growing federal deficits will likely drive up the state’s borrowing costs. Counties are scrambling to fill gaps in once-federally funded services. Lieutenant Governor Eleni Kounalakis’ record in business, politics and diplomacy is public and extensive. There are few blanks to fill in. The challenges awaiting the next treasurer require a tested policy and economic operator.  That is Lt. Gov. Kounalakis.

(Our interview with Eleni Kounalakis is here. Jennifer Hawks would not complete our interview request or provide her resume after repeated requests.)

Insurance commissioner — Ben Allen

Bay Area voters must understand: When they choose termed-out Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara’s successor, the viability of homeownership is what’s really on the ballot. The conditions for deadlier and most destructive wildfires keep growing. And there is no end in sight. There is only one candidate who grasps the complexity of this crisis and has the legislative experience to address it. That is Ben Allen.

(Our interviews with insurance commissioner candidates Ben Allen and Jane Kim are here.)

Superintendent of Public Instruction — Richard Barrera

California schools face a constellation of challenges, including declining enrollment, shrinking budgets and persistent achievement gaps. Richard Barrera is a detail-oriented incrementalist focused on student absenteeism and mental health. His opponent, Sonja Shaw, is a brash culture warrior more interested in girls’ locker rooms than the nitty gritty of school reform. We support Barrera.

(Our interviews with Richard Barrera and Sonja Shaw are here and here, respectively.)

Prop. 38 — No

This deceptive ballot measure is an $8.4 billion money grab that would funnel taxpayer funds to a single private medical institute run by a billionaire, who is also the chief backer of the Proposition 38 campaign.

Prop. 39 — No

Our editorial board repeatedly asked the Yes on Proposition 39 campaign for evidence of voter fraud in California, rampant or otherwise, and they deflected, dodged and denied the premise of our questions. In requiring voters to show IDs before receiving their ballots (among other restrictive voting rules and audits), it gives credence to President Donald Trump’s longstanding and repeatedly debunked claims that millions of noncitizens routinely vote in U.S. elections. This initiative is an expensive solution to a phantom problem. Reject Proposition 39.

Prop. 40 — No

Prop. 41— No

Prop. 42 — No

Prop. 43 — Yes

California voters have twice approved initiatives making it harder to raise local government taxes — only to have the courts erode them. Proposition 43 would undo the latest judicial meddling by once again restoring a supermajority threshold for passage of special taxes. Voters should approve it.

Prop. 44 — No

Proposition 44 would require California’s 2,000 safety net clinics to spend at least 90% of their revenue on patient care and the services that support it, and to report publicly how their money is spent. On the surface, that sounds great. However, if passed, the measure would push the majority of clinics across California into the red — many of which have spent the last six years clawing their way back from the brink of the pandemic.

Prop. 45 —Yes

Prop. 45 won’t transform the state’s regulatory maze overnight. But it would make California’s planning approval process fairer, less expensive and considerably more predictable.

Measure RTM (aka Bay Area’s Regional Transit Measure) —  No

Bay Area voters, threatened with a transportation apocalypse if they don’t approve a billion-dollar-a-year sales tax increase for public transit, should just say no. Measure RTM on the Nov. 3 ballot would add 0.5% to the cost of goods for the next 14 years in Alameda, Contra Costa, Santa Clara and San Mateo counties and 1% in San Francisco. Opposing the initiative is not a vote against public transit. It’s a demand for responsible financial management.

U.S. Congress

Congressional District 14 — Aisha Wahab

Among the two candidates left vying for ex-Rep. Eric Swalwell’s seat, Rep. Aisha Wahab is the best choice to represent Alameda County. The U.S. Congress is no place to send a novice. Former state Senator Wahab isn’t one. As the assistant majority leader, she was the third most powerful lawmaker in the California Senate. In that chamber, the 39-year-old Democrat had a front-row seat to the affordability debates affecting this district, which includes Hayward, Fremont, Castro Valley and Livermore. There is only one reliable, proven candidate in this race. That’s Wahab. (Wahab has represented the East Bay in Congress since September after defeating Melissa Hernandez in an August runoff to temporarily occupy Swalwell’s vacant seat. The Nov. 3 election will determine who will permanently represent the district.)

(Our interviews with candidates running to replace ex-Rep. Eric Swalwell — Aisha Wahab and Melissa Hernandez — are here.)

San Jose City Council

District 5 — Peter Ortiz

District 7 — Bien Doan

District 9 — Genny Altwer

This district in the southwest corner of San Jose – which includes Cambrian Park, Hillsdale, Kirk Park and parts of Willow Glen – does not have the same degree of vacant storefronts, job losses, blight and homelessness as other parts of this city. But it is not immune. Whoever replaces Vice Mayor Pam Foley must be able to address this 100,000-person district’s concerns while helping solve citywide problems. We believe that candidate is Genny Altwer. Altwer, 45, represents neither the status quo nor a radical break from it. Her unconventional resume should bring new thinking to old policies.

(Our interviews with candidates Genny Altwer and Gordon Chester are here.)

Palo Alto

Measure J — No

The Cubberley Community Center in Palo Alto, at roughly 70 years old, is desperately in need of an upgrade. It’s clear that this city’s 67,000 residents deserve a fully functional, modern public space. However, Measure J — a forever tax written to deceive voters into funding a project whose ultimate cost isn’t even known to its backers — is not the way to do it. As we see it, Measure J is a quintessential example of lying by omission.
