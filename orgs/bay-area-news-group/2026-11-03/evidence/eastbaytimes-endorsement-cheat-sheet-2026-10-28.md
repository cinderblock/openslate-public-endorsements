Endorsement Cheat Sheet: Mercury News and East Bay Times Editorial Boards’ picks for California’s Nov. 3 general election

Read our explanations for how we reached positions on the most competitive races and significant measures on Bay Area voters' ballots

By Mercury News & East Bay Times Editorial Boards

PUBLISHED: October 13, 2026 at 8:22 AM PDT | UPDATED: October 27, 2026 at 4:29 PM PDT

Here are our recommendations, so far, for the most competitive races and significant ballot measures on Bay Area voters’ Nov. 3 ballots. To reach our conclusions, we have interviewed more than 70 candidates for federal, state, county, city and school district offices as well as the leaders of committees supporting and opposing initiatives. Please know that, per company policy, we do not endorse in gubernatorial races; and given editorial resources, we prioritize making endorsements in open-seat, or non-incumbent, races. Bookmark this page to view additional endorsements as they are published between now and election day.

Statewide offices

Lieutenant governor — Fiona Ma

Success as this state’s No. 2 requires understanding its levers of power. Currently treasurer, Fiona Ma already knows how to leverage them. The next lieutenant governor must be as capable of navigating uncharted waters as the governor whom she or he might need to replace at a moment’s notice. By that standard, Fiona Ma is eminently qualified.

(Our interviews with the two candidates for lieutenant governor — Fiona Ma and Gloria Romero — are here.)

Treasurer — Eleni Kounalakis

California’s next treasurer should expect to inherit the most financially volatile environment since the Great Recession. The state faces long-running structural shortfalls. Growing federal deficits will likely drive up the state’s borrowing costs. Counties are scrambling to fill gaps in once-federally funded services. Lieutenant Governor Eleni Kounalakis’ record in business, politics and diplomacy is public and extensive. There are few blanks to fill in. The challenges awaiting the next treasurer require a tested policy and economic operator. That is Lt. Gov. Kounalakis.

(Our interview with Eleni Kounalakis is here. Jennifer Hawks would not complete our interview request or provide her resume after repeated requests.)

Insurance commissioner — Ben Allen

Bay Area voters must understand: When they choose termed-out Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara’s successor, the viability of homeownership is what’s really on the ballot. The conditions for deadlier and most destructive wildfires keep growing. And there is no end in sight. There is only one candidate who grasps the complexity of this crisis and has the legislative experience to address it. That is Ben Allen.

(Our interviews with insurance commissioner candidates Ben Allen and Jane Kim are here.)

Superintendent of Public Instruction — Richard Barrera

California schools face a constellation of challenges, including declining enrollment, shrinking budgets and persistent achievement gaps. Richard Barrera is a detail-oriented incrementalist focused on student absenteeism and mental health. His opponent, Sonja Shaw, is a brash culture warrior more interested in girls’ locker rooms than the nitty gritty of school reform. We support Barrera.

(Our interviews with Richard Barrera and Sonja Shaw are here and here, respectively.)

California ballot measures

Prop. 1 — No

This initiative puts state taxpayers in debt for 25 years and pumps money into a bloated system. Under the best of circumstances, it would add a tiny fraction of the new housing supply needed all while doing next to nothing to solve the real problem: the inexcusably exorbitant cost of building affordable housing.

Prop. 2 — Yes

California’s budget rules perversely count saving as spending, limiting state Legislators’ capacities to bank windfalls for leaner years. That’s fiscal malpractice written into the Constitution. Proposition 2 would exempt rainy day fund deposits from the Gann Limit, double the fund’s cap and require larger deposits when capital gains revenues surge. A state with volatile tax revenues needs a bigger cushion, not obstacles to saving.

Prop. 3 — Yes

California’s richest are doing great. Over the last four decades, while the bottom four-fifths of households have seen their real incomes decline, the top 1% have seen their real incomes grow two and half times. This upper stratum now earns in a week what the middle fifth of Californians make in a year. Proposition 3 asks the Californians truly living the California dream — the highest 2% of earners — to keep paying their fair share while also helping the state avoid painful cuts to services induced by President Trump’s and House Republicans’ deliberately punitive H.R. 1, or the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Prop. 4 — No

Taxpayer dollars must stay reserved to fix municipal roads and parks, rather than finance elections.

Prop. 5 — No

Changing the California Constitution in a way that creates even bigger obstacles to electing independent-minded candidates — while stripping the people of their primary tool of accountability — is a bad idea.

Prop. 37 — No

This measure looks great on the surface. But all that glitters isn’t gold. It would give builders an escape hatch, if not a bonanza. Instead of having to lower prices, it would artificially inflate the market by putting borrowed cash into buyers’ hands. Instead of lowering construction costs, this merely helps California home buyers take on more debt in the form of second mortgages.

Prop. 38 — No

This deceptive ballot measure is an $8.4 billion money grab that would funnel taxpayer funds to a single private medical institute run by a billionaire, who is also the chief backer of the Proposition 38 campaign.

Prop. 39 — No

Our editorial board repeatedly asked the Yes on Proposition 39 campaign for evidence of voter fraud in California, rampant or otherwise, and they deflected, dodged and denied the premise of our questions. In requiring voters to show IDs before receiving their ballots (among other restrictive voting rules and audits), it gives credence to President Donald Trump’s longstanding and repeatedly debunked claims that millions of noncitizens routinely vote in U.S. elections. This initiative is an expensive solution to a phantom problem. Reject Proposition 39.

Prop. 40 — No

Proposition 40 was written to prop up this state’s safety net in the face of these devastating cuts. However, this measure is the wrong solution to a real problem. We advise California voters to reject short-sighted, self-sabotaging measure — the ballot equivalent of eating your seed corn.

Prop. 41— No

The problem with Proposition 41 is its second provision. It would prevent any new special taxes from being excluded from the “Gann Limit” — a strict state spending cap enacted in 1979. That means that voters could approve a new tax to address a pressing public need, only to discover that the state cannot spend the money it collects without being constrained by a constitutional limit written half a century ago. California has changed radically since the 1970s. And its fiscal rules must leave the state with the flexibility to address issues voters of the era never had to deal with — six-figure college costs, seven-figure mortgages and 13-figure wildfire costs. The state must not surrender its future freedom of action to defeat a single, one-time tax proposal.

Prop. 42 — No

Proposition 42 was written by this tech boom’s beneficiaries, who are now among the wealthiest humans in history. If this initiative had limited itself to nullifying Proposition 40, we could have supported it. However, amid this era’s technology- and climate-induced uncertainty, California must keep its options open. This measure would needlessly and recklessly narrow them.

Prop. 43 — Yes

California voters have twice approved initiatives making it harder to raise local government taxes — only to have the courts erode them. Proposition 43 would undo the latest judicial meddling by once again restoring a supermajority threshold for passage of special taxes. Voters should approve it.

Prop. 44 — No

Proposition 44 would require California’s 2,000 safety net clinics to spend at least 90% of their revenue on patient care and the services that support it, and to report publicly how their money is spent. On the surface, that sounds great. However, if passed, the measure would push the majority of clinics across California into the red — many of which have spent the last six years clawing their way back from the brink of the pandemic.

Prop. 45 —Yes

Proposition 45 won’t transform the state’s regulatory maze overnight. But it would make California’s planning approval process fairer, less expensive and considerably more predictable.

Bay Area transit tax

Measure RTM — No

Bay Area voters, threatened with a transportation apocalypse if they don’t approve a billion-dollar-a-year sales tax increase for public transit, should just say no. Measure RTM on the Nov. 3 ballot would add 0.5% to the cost of goods for the next 14 years in Alameda, Contra Costa, Santa Clara and San Mateo counties and 1% in San Francisco. Opposing the initiative is not a vote against public transit. It’s a demand for responsible financial management.

U.S. Congress

Congressional District 14 — Aisha Wahab

Among the two candidates left vying for ex-Rep. Eric Swalwell’s seat, Rep. Aisha Wahab is the best choice to represent Alameda County. The U.S. Congress is no place to send a novice. Former state Senator Wahab isn’t one. As the assistant majority leader, she was the third most powerful lawmaker in the California Senate. In that chamber, the 39-year-old Democrat had a front-row seat to the affordability debates affecting this district, which includes Hayward, Fremont, Castro Valley and Livermore. There is only one reliable, proven candidate in this race. That’s Wahab. (Wahab has represented the East Bay in Congress since September after defeating Melissa Hernandez in an August runoff to temporarily occupy Swalwell’s vacant seat. The Nov. 3 election will determine who will permanently represent the district.)

(Our interviews with candidates running to replace ex-Rep. Eric Swalwell — Aisha Wahab and Melissa Hernandez — are here.)

California Legislature

State Senate District 10 — Scott Sakakihara

The Union City councilmember is a thoughtful policy wonk who provided our editorial board with detailed answers to the district’s biggest challenges: housing costs, homelessness, struggling schools, fragmented transit systems, etc. Sakakihara — a Harvard-educated US Navy Reserve officer, Democrat and former Palantir executive — explained how he would speed housing approvals, strengthen student mental health services and demand results for taxpayers’ money. His Republican opponent, Linda Price, remains a relative unknown. Price refused multiple requests to provide her resume or answer our questionnaire. Send Sakakihara to represent this diverse, million-person district, which includes Hayward, Newark and Fremont in the north and Santa Clara, Sunnyvale and San Jose in the south.

(Our interview with Scott Sakakihara is here.)

San Jose City Council

District 5 — Peter Ortiz

District 7 — Bien Doan

District 9 — Genny Altwer

This district in the southwest corner of San Jose – which includes Cambrian Park, Hillsdale, Kirk Park and parts of Willow Glen – does not have the same degree of vacant storefronts, job losses, blight and homelessness as other parts of this city. But it is not immune. Whoever replaces Vice Mayor Pam Foley must be able to address this 100,000-person district’s concerns while helping solve citywide problems. We believe that candidate is Genny Altwer. Altwer, 45, represents neither the status quo nor a radical break from it. Her unconventional resume should bring new thinking to old policies.

(Our interviews with candidates Genny Altwer and Gordon Chester are here.)

San Jose Unified School District

Area 4 — Michael Melillo

Palo Alto

Measure J — No

The Cubberley Community Center in Palo Alto, at roughly 70 years old, is desperately in need of an upgrade. It’s clear that this city’s 67,000 residents deserve a fully functional, modern public space. However, Measure J — a forever tax written to deceive voters into funding a project whose ultimate cost isn’t even known to its backers — is not the way to do it. As we see it, Measure J is a quintessential example of lying by omission.

San Mateo County

Board of Supervisors, District 5 — Mark Nagales

South San Francisco Councilman Mark Nagales’ experience serving this 150-person district, his command of policy, his attention to detail and his ability to assemble a governing majority convince us he is voters’ best option to navigate San Mateo County’s challenges.

Concord

City Council District 1 — Laura Hoffmeister

City Council District 3 — Dena Acosta-Beere

City Council District 5 — Laura Nakamura

Oakland

Measure EE — No

The last thing Oakland needs is a politicized city administration with the mayor running the show. Instead, it needs a professional city manager who is accountable to the mayor and council members. There’s no doubt the city’s current structure is broken. But Mayor Barbara Lee’s scheme would exacerbate the problems rather than fix them.
