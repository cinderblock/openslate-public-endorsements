Proposition 45 threatens public health and environmental protections that protect our air, water, and communities. The measure weakens existing environmental protections, while making it harder for the public to challenge harmful projects, such as data centers and power plants. Californians deserve clean air, safe water, and a voice that is heard in decisions that affect our health and future.
Proposition 45, if passed, would make sweeping changes to the state’s bedrock environmental protection, the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). This law requires state and local governments to review the total environmental impacts of projects before they can be approved. CEQA is widely enforced, everything from housing development projects to data centers go through the review process. It also gives the public a say in the environmental review process.
The goal of Prop 45 is to fast track specific projects deemed as “essential.” However, because of how Prop 45 is written, what is considered “essential” is so broad, it may apply to: data centers, power plants, roads, freeways, dams, canals, landfill gas, and any project that arguably reduces wildlife fire risks.

 Data centers that create pollution could be fast tracked under Prop 45
Prop 45 reverses the foundational understanding of Environmental Review
Prop 45 asserts that completion of these fast tracked projects is the priority of the environmental review process. Not the environment.
The current understanding is that environmental review should be applied to protect the environment to the maximum extent possible. This reversal, especially in combination with the broad list of projects that Prop 45 could apply to, would undermine what has made environmental review work so well to protect our air and water: the current law works to protect our environment against developer interests.
Prop 45 makes it nearly impossible to prevent a bad project
Right now, anyone can still sue a project if they believe the environmental review process was not done correctly or missed valuable information. If the courts agreed, the project could be stopped.
Prop 45 changes this drastically. It prevents the public from stopping these projects once they are approved. Now, even if the court found that a project was approved, but poses more significant environmental damages than originally reported or even poses a large public safety risk, they cannot stop the project entirely.
But if anyone found that the project would harm our clean air, water or wildlife, there would be little they could do to stop it.
Prop 45 also raises serious concerns about the financial burden on taxpayers.
According to the Legislative Analyst’s Office, the measure could cost taxpayers as much as $100 million annually. If polluters face fewer obligations to address potential contamination, the public could be left paying to clean up pollution and manage its consequences. Californians shouldn’t have to sacrifice their health or their tax dollars because polluters are no longer held accountable.
