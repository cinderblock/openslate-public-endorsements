At first blush, Proposition 39 seems to be a simple proposal — require voters to provide identification when they cast ballots. Of the 50 states, plus Washington, D.C, and Puerto Rico, 36 require ID — 24 of those require photo ID and 12 accept non-photo ID. California is among the 16 not requiring ID.

Instead, California asks voters to sign in when voting in-person. Voter signatures and other information must match elections office files. For those voting by mail — and that’s most Californians — voters must sign the outside of the return envelope. The signature is compared to the one on file at the elections office before the ballot is counted.

So, requiring California voters to show their IDs when they vote in-person, or put some other ID proof on the outside of an envelope when voting by mail doesn’t seem to be a “big deal.” That is, unless the required ID is difficult for some voters to obtain.

If Proposition 39 had stopped at simply requiring voters to provide easily available ID proof when they cast ballots, likely there would be little controversy.

But there is much more to Proposition 39. And to understand that, voters must consider the difference between “registering” to vote and actually voting.

To register, voters must be 18 years old, live in the election’s jurisdiction, not be imprisoned, or otherwise legally disqualified, and be a U.S. citizen. San Francisco and Oakland allow noncitizens to vote in some school board elections.

To register, some forms of ID are required. As to citizenship, voters sign — under penalty of perjury — that they are citizens. If noncitizens lie and vote, they can be prosecuted, jailed and deported. The same registration procedure is used in most states. Despite what President Trump and others have said, noncitizens voting in California and throughout the country is very rare.

Proposition 39 would require state and local elections officials to use their “best efforts” to verify the U.S. citizenship status of the voters they register. The state auditor must audit state and county elections offices for compliance every two years. County offices must report annually their verification progress.

It’s routine for elections offices in California and other states to continually do “maintenance audits” — culling out voters who have died, moved or otherwise are no longer eligible to vote.

But removing, or identifying voters on the basis of their citizenship status is complicated and error-prone. There is no comprehensive federal or state list of U.S. citizens. Some federal lists, including those the Trump administration proposed to use to restrict mail voting, mostly include the immigration status of immigrants, not the existence of U.S.-born citizens living in the country.

The president’s SAVE act, which proposes stricter proof of citizenship to vote, has been stalled in the Republican-controlled Congress, and blocked in federal court over constitutional concerns and the potential for U.S. citizens to be misidentified and denied the right to vote. State efforts to require voters to provide proof of citizenship have mostly been blocked by courts on similar grounds.

The lack of reliable government databases to prove citizenship and document immigration status has been one reason employers have long resisted using the federal E-Verify system to verify employee and job applicant citizenship in compliance with hiring laws.

The California Legislative Analyst's Office concluded Proposition 39 would increase annual state and local government costs “by tens of millions of dollars to low hundreds of millions of dollars.”

But the cost goes beyond money. Elections staffs will be burdened with checking voter immigration status at the expense of what they should be focusing on — accurately and efficiently administering elections and counting votes. And use of unreliable citizenship databases could cost untold numbers of citizens the right to vote.

Vote NO on Proposition 39.
