A 2017 ruling by the California Supreme Court upended that concept, opening up a loophole in long-established law and inviting countless special interest groups to place taxing propositions on election ballots.

The result was that voter-proposed “special” taxes, which have been collecting millions of dollars from taxpayers, only need a simple majority of votes to pass.

A yes on Proposition 43 will restore voters’ long-expressed intent to control the imposition of all “special” new taxes, by requiring a two-thirds vote of the people to pass them.

This is not to say that the taxing ballot measures that have managed to squeeze through the 2017 loophole have been unworthy. Many have funded much-needed public services. But voters decided decades ago that layering one tax on top of another should require greater voter scrutiny.

To understand Proposition 43, you need to recall passage of Proposition 13 in 1978. Fed up with paying skyrocketing property taxes, voters passed landmark taxing controls on government.

Proposition 13 is mostly remembered for its curbs on property tax hikes. But it also set limits on how “special” and “general” government taxes could be established, extended and increased.

“General” taxes are proposed to fund the general operation of government. They only require a simple majority vote to pass.

As an example, Bakersfield’s Measure N, which passed in 2018 by only 97 votes and levies an extra 1 percent on the city’s sales tax rate, is a general tax, with about $100 million raised annually to fund general city expenses.

The campaign to pass the measure listed suggested uses — police, fire, homelessness, etc. — but it did not include an obligation to actually spend the money on those things. Instead, Measure N included a citizen’s oversight committee to monitor and propose how the money should be spent.

A “special” tax is one obligated to a specific use — fire, police, roads, parks, public transit, etc. Those require two-thirds of the voters to agree to enact them.

In 1996, voters again restated their desire to require “special” taxes to have at least two-thirds voter support. They passed Proposition 218, which placed the requirement in the state constitution and defined “special” taxes as a government tax imposed for a special purpose. It also extended the requirement to other districts — special, school and community college — as well as real estate parcel taxes.

But along comes the California Supreme Court in 2017 to open a loophole in a law that before had seemed pretty straightforward.

The loophole was established in the case of California Cannabis Coalition versus the City of Upland. The city attempted to block from the ballot the coalition’s legalization measure, which also included a regulatory fee, or tax, schedule. The city contended, among other things, the measure did not comply with the Proposition 13 and Proposition 218 “special” tax criteria.

In its ruling, the court noted that the “local government” referred to in propositions 13 and 218 that required a two-thirds vote may not have included the coalition, because the coalition was not a government agency proposing the tax. Rather, it was “voters exercising their initiative power.”

Subsequently, other courts concurred that non-government, special interest groups — labor unions, industry, environmental groups, etc. — could qualify “special” taxes for the ballot and those measures would only need a simple majority vote to pass. Some “voter” tax measures have been placed on ballots to carried out in the interest of the more strictly regulated government agencies.

If Proposition 43 passes, beginning in 2027, a two-thirds vote of the people will be required to pass all local special taxes. It will apply to taxes proposed by government agencies, as well as “voter initiatives.”

And that is how it should be. Voters twice have expressed their desire to closely limit the layering on of new special taxes by requiring a two-thirds vote.
