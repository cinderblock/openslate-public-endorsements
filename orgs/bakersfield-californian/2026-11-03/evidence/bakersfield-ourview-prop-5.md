Proposition 5 was placed on the Nov. 3 ballot by a vote of the Legislature after the failed attempt to recall Gov. Gavin Newsom in 2021. It proposes to change the way Californians simultaneously recall and replace state officers in the same election.
Under current law, if citizens collect sufficient signatures on petitions to qualify a recall measure for the ballot, voters are asked two questions: Should the official be recalled (removed from office) and who should replace them?

The candidate receiving the most votes — which may not be a majority of votes — takes office as the replacement. The incumbent targeted by the recall cannot run as a “replacement” candidate.

Proposition 5 proposes to separate the recall from the replacement election. The official being recalled also will be able to compete in the later replacement election.

In the case of governor, for example, voters first would decide if the official should be recalled. If voters say yes, then the lieutenant governor would assume the office until a later special election is held and voters cast ballots to select a replacement.

A primary duty of the lieutenant governor, who is independently elected statewide, is to step into the governor’s post when the governor is unable to serve. Being recalled is certainly a reason for not being able to serve. The governor and lieutenant governor are not elected as “a ticket.” They may or may not even be members of the same party.

Proposition 5 will give voters a better opportunity to thoughtfully and separately consider two important questions — should an official be recalled and who should be the replacement.

The current system rushes consideration of both questions. It also can result in a replacement candidate assuming an office with less than the majority of votes casts.

Consider the failed recall of Gov. Newsom. Less than 52% of the state’s eligible voters cast ballots in the off-year, off-timed election and 46 candidates competed to replace him. An overwhelming 62% of voters refused to recall Newsom. But if the recall had prevailed, Republican radio talk show host Larry Elder would have been California’s next governor after receiving only about 48% of the replacement votes. He was the top vote-getter.

According to the Secretary of State’s Office, between 1913 and 2025, there have been 182 recall attempts of state officials, with only 11 qualifying for the ballot. Only six succeeded.

The only successful recall of a governor was Gray Davis in 2003. With 135 candidates qualified to appear on the ballot, only four received at least 1% of the vote. Less than a majority vote — 48.6% — was all that it took to replace Davis with celebrity Arnold Schwarzenegger.

If the recall and replacement elections had been separated, would California voters have elected as their governor a candidate who had no political experience and a public persona built on acting and body building? It’s hard to say.

But the reform proposed in Proposition 5 will give replacement candidates a better chance to vigorously compete in a traditionally conducted special election, with voters better able to really focus on the candidates’ qualifications, experience and ability to lead California.

The current system is flawed. It needs to be changed. Vote yes on Proposition 5.
