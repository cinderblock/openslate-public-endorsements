The Californian struggled with its 22nd Congressional District election recommendation. Likely, many district voters also will struggle to decide who will best represent them. There are plenty of good reasons for voting for the Republican incumbent congressman, David Valadao, or for his opponent, Randy Villegas. Among them is just having an “R” or “D” after the candidate’s name and the desire to flip control of the House to Democrats.

But if you are a voter who believes your elected representative should stand for what you stand for, and should put your interests and well-being ahead of his party’s interests, you may have concerns about your 22nd CD choices.

Voters’ passage last year of Proposition 50 significantly changed the district’s boundaries and composition. It now sprawls over five counties, rather than the original three. It has more Democratic registered voters and more minority residents. Proposition 50 was California’s response to President Trump’s demand that Red states redraw their congressional boundary lines ahead of the midterm elections to reduce the number of House seats represented by Democrats and increase the number represented by Republicans. It’s a game being played between Republicans and Democrats for control of Congress. Voters are just pawns.

How the system is supposed to work: Following each decennial U.S. census, a citizens’ committee holds hearings, crunches numbers and draws political boundary lines that keep neighborhoods, communities and shared interest in basically the same district. That process in 2021 resulted in the 22nd Congressional District being a relatively compact three-county area that included mostly Kern, as well as Tulare and Kings counties. It now stretches to include mostly Kern, as well as parts of Kings, Tulare, Fresno and Madera counties.

Valadao has represented the 22nd Congressional District for years. He has defied logic and odds by getting repeatedly reelected in a district that has historically been dominated by Democratic voters, a Latino population and low-income people. There’s a lot of speculation how he has managed to do that. The district’s Latino population has a relatively low voting rate. Latinos are not a monolithic voting bloc. Many are politically conservative and that makes the moderate farmer from Hanford fairly popular. Valadao’s low-key-keep-your-head-down “brand” also helps.

But is Valadao a “good fit” for the district?

While Kern remains the biggest part of the district, Republican-voting Kings County, where Valadao lives, is a smaller part. And the addition of Fresno has added about 100,000 people to the district. Likely, many are urban Democrats. But, amazingly, with all this sleight of hand, pundits still regard the district a toss-up. They are not ruling out Valadao’s reelection.

Why? Because he just may be a better fit than his opponent, Democrat Villegas. Those who think Valadao will be defeated simply by Democrats tweaking a few boundary lines don’t understand the valley, nor the 22nd Congresional District.

There’s good reason Central Valley Democrats often are called “Valleycrats.” They are moderately conservative politicians, who represent a lot of no-nonsense working-class voters. An example is Fresno’s long-serving Democratic Congressman Jim Costa. He’s not perfect; but he’s their guy. He shares their Portuguese immigrant roots. He’s not afraid to buck his fellow Democrats when it comes to standing up for his hometown folks.

That brings us to Villegas, Valadao’s Democratic challenger. The two men have some things in common. Both have immigrant parents. Villegas’ were from Mexico; Valadao’s from the Azores. Both entered politics for personal reasons: Valadao because of water issues affecting his family farm; Villegas because he knew first-hand the challenge and heartbreak of broken immigration policies, healthcare shortages and poverty.

Born and raised in Bakersfield, Villegas earned a bachelor’s degree from Cal State Bakersfield and a doctorate from UC Santa Cruz. He was the first in his family to go to college. Along the way, he was a journalist and community organizer in Kern County. He now teaches political science at College of the Sequoias and is an elected member of the Visalia Unified School District Board of Trustees.

But is Villegas’ progressive politics a fit for the valley and district voters who have long supported moderate politicians — Democrats and Republicans? They expect their representative to fight as hard for the worker pulling grapes from vineyards, as he would for the one pulling oil from a well; who values both alternative energy and fossil fuel; and who respects the needs of corporate and small farmers.

Villegas looks years ahead to when we may have Medicare for all, but 22nd Congressional District residents want affordable health insurance today. Progressive U.S. Sen. Bernie Sanders recently came to Bakersfield to fire up Villegas’ base. But did Sanders deliver a message voters wanted to hear? We will know on Election Day.

While we are disappointed that Valadao isn’t really as independent as he claims to be, he is a better fit for the valley and 22nd District. He should be reelected.

But whoever wins this up-for-grabs election must remember who sent them to Congress. It wasn’t their political parties. It wasn’t President Trump. It wasn’t progressives in Washington, D.C. It was their folks back home.

### VINCE FONG

20TH CONGRESS

The 20th Congressional District is in the southern San Joaquin Valley and includes much of Bakersfield and the surrounding agricultural communities. The district also includes portions of Fresno, Kings and Tulare counties. Nearly 50% of the district is white, non-Hispanic, and about 34% Hispanic or Latino. About 47% of voters are registered as Republican, with about 26% registered as Democratic and about 19% having no party preference.

Republican Vince Fong has represented the district in Congress since 2024, when he won a special election to fill former Republican Rep. Kevin McCarthy’s remaining term, after McCarthy resigned his seat. Fong also was elected to a separate two-year term.

Raised in Bakersfield, Fong graduated from West High School in 1997, earned a bachelor’s degree from UCLA in 2001, and a master’s degree in public administration from Princeton University in 2003. He has spent most of his career in politics and public service in Kern County. He worked on the staffs of former Congressmen Bill Thomas and McCarthy. From 2016 to 2024, he represented Bakersfield’s 34th Assembly District.

In Congress, he has served on committees including Homeland Security; Science, Space and Technology; and Transportation and Infrastructure. His priorities have included oil, gas and energy independence; water and agriculture; immigration and border security; aerospace and national defense; Second Amendment gun rights; lower taxes; and reducing business regulations.

Democrat Sandra Van Scotter opposes his reelection. A first-time candidate for public elective office, Van Scotter lives in Ridgecrest. She has spent more than 20 years working in healthcare, including as a respiratory therapist in hospitals. Currently, she assists adults with intellectual and development disabilities.

She says her grassroots campaign focuses on bringing better constituent services to the district, which she believes has been neglected by the incumbent. She says her years of experience in healthcare have shown her how federal policies can adversely affect individuals and families.

Fong and Van Scotter emerged from the June primary election as the top-two vote getters. Fong received 68.2% of the vote and Van Scotter received 28.8%.

There is little doubt that Fong will be — and should be — reelected to continue serving as the 20th Congressional District representative. But citizens willing to step forward and compete in campaigns, even when the odds are against them, help keep incumbent politicians from becoming complacent and unresponsive. Van Scotter is thanked for her campaign.
