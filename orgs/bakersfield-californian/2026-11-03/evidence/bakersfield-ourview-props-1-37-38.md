Voters will decide the fate of three bond initiatives on the Nov. 3 ballot. Two initiatives — Propositions 1 and 37 — address California’s affordable housing shortage — and Proposition 38 proposes to use state bonds to fund medical research.
Voters should vote YES on Propositions 1 and 37, but vote NO on Proposition 38.

In September, the U.S. Census Bureau released its latest poverty calculations. California was tied with Louisiana for having the nation’s highest poverty rate. A major factor was California’s high housing costs.

State bonds act in ways similar to home mortgages. To obtain upfront money for programs, the state sells bonds to investors and pays off the loan, with interest, over several years — often 25 years.

The bond system spares the state’s general fund from taking an initial big hit. But it cost taxpayers more in the long run to “retire” the debt, with interest.

There are basically two types of bonds — general obligation, which pay back investors using general funds (tax dollars); and revenue bonds, which are paid back by user fees. An example is using revenue bonds to build a bridge, with investors paid back from the tolls users pay to cross it.

### Proposition 1

Placed on the ballot by the Legislature, Proposition 1 would authorize $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds to finance affordable housing and homeownership programs.

The bond money would be allocated to finance:

• Construction and preservation of affordable rental housing for low-income Californians.

• Programs to help eligible Californians purchase homes.

• Assistance to veterans seeking affordable homeownership.

Supporters include, among others, labor unions, veterans, teachers, the California Apartment Association and Chamber of Commerce, disability rights groups, League of Women Voters, Habitat for Humanity, and Gov. Gavin Newsom and other state political leaders.

Supporters contend public financing is needed to build homes for people who cannot afford market-rate prices. The additional housing created through the bonds will be a permanent investment that will serve generations of Californians for many decades.

### Proposition 37

Placed on the ballot as a “citizen’s initiative,” Proposition 37 is sponsored by real estate and labor interests to create a new state homebuying loan program.

Proposition 37 authorizes up to $25 billion in state revenue bonds to be sold to investors to finance partial loans to help fund down payments to qualify Californians to buy new homes. The initiative’s proponents note that the down payment is one of the biggest barriers to buying a home in California.

Here’s how it would work: Investor money would flow through the California Housing Finance Agency. A qualifying homebuyer would have to put at least 3% down to apply for up to a 17% loan. Together, the money would go toward a 20% down payment on a private mortgage. Basically, it would be a secondary loan on the purchase, with the mortgage being the primary.

To qualify for the loan, the buyer’s household income could be no more than 200% of the area’s median income. The house must be newly constructed. Depending on the county, there may be a limit on the selling price. Repayment of the revenue bonds will come from homeowners’ mortgage payments, not the state’s general fund.

Proposition 37 also contains incentives for builders. In exchange for agreeing to certain labor requirements, builders will operate under “different rules” concerning construction-defect lawsuits.

There are many unknowns about Proposition 37. Who will benefit most from the program and what will the impact on marketplace prices be if down payment help is available? Will it only help upper middle-class buyers? Since only new homes will qualify, will it discourage urban infill and encourage sprawl? Will buyers be taking on loans they can’t pay back? Will concession made to builders leave buyers at a disadvantage? Will investors want to buy the revenue bonds since their “loans” are secondary and more at risk if buyers default? Those are all good questions. Only time will provide the answers.

But California’s high housing costs are squeezing many people out of the market. Proposition 37 is a worthwhile effort to help. It is a good addition to a more comprehensive plan to create affordable housing.

### Proposition 38

Placed on the ballot as a “citizen’s initiative,” Proposition 38 would authorize the state to borrow $8.4 billion through the sale of general obligation bonds to fund medical research focused on immunology and immunotherapy. The bond money would be allocated:

• $4.2 billion to a single California research institute selected by the California Public Health Department that specializes in immunology and immunotherapy.

• $4.2 billion in competitive grants to California universities and nonprofit organizations specializing in the same field.

Overall, at least $4.2 billion of the total must be directed to research involving cancer, heart disease and Alzheimer’s disease.

The state would borrow the money from investors and pay it back, with interest, from the general fund. According to the Legislative Analyst Office, repayment is estimated to cost about $500 million to $600 million a year over 25 years.

Proponents argue that Proposition 38 funding is needed because recent federal funding cuts and uncertainty are disrupting promising research on innovative medical treatments.

The Secretary of State’s office lists California physician Dr. Gary K. Michelson as the sole proponent of Proposition 38. Listed in the Voter Guide as principal supporters are the Michael J. Fox Foundation, Alzheimer’s Los Angeles, Kidney Cancer Association and the California Nurses Association. Listed as opponent is Robert M. Kaplan, a Stanford senior scholar. The League of Women Voters also opposes Proposition 38.

Michelson is a co-founder and chairman of the board of the California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy, a UCLA-affiliated nonprofit organization. He and his institute’s co-founder, Meyer Luskin, are the primary donors to the Proposition 38 campaign.

In a lengthy July article analyzing Proposition 38, news outlet CalMatters noted that the criteria included in the initiative’s text appears to single out Michelson’s institute as the only one that would qualify to receive $4.2 billion — or half — of the bond money.

It seems unwise to concentrate these research dollars on one institute, when multiple public and private organizations also are working in the same field. But the structure of the allocation process is not critics’ only concern. The narrow focus of research slated to benefit and the barriers for other researchers to compete for the money is a focus of criticism.

While funding medical research with general obligation bonds is not unprecedented, it is unusual. General obligation bonds are usually used to finance infrastructure projects, such as roads, bridges, housing, etc. These projects serve communities and Californians for decades — often beyond the time it takes to pay off the bonds, or debt.

Funding research through general obligation bonds is quite different. Money is used upfront for grants, salaries and operating costs, with no guarantee that there will be anything to show from the research. Bond money used in early years may be used up, with research discontinued — possibly replaced my new innovative developments. But taxpayers still would be obligated to keep paying for the remaining years of the 25-year debt.

While funding for medical research is necessary, Proposition 38 and general obligation bonds are not the appropriate funding mechanism.
