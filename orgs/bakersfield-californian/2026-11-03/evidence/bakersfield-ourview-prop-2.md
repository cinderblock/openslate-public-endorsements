Penetrating the fog of the state’s budget is a tough slog for Californians under the “best circumstances.” But when they are asked to vote on 14 consequential initiatives on a single ballot, it’s not the best time to understand state finances.
Placed on the Nov. 3 ballot by the Legislature, Proposition 2, or “Save for California’s Future Act,” basically would do three things:

• Increase the size of the state’s “rainy-day fund” from 10% to 20% of general fund revenues.

• Require the state to make deposits into its reserves until it reaches the higher limit.

• Extend requirements for making extra payments to retire some state debts to 2040. The existing requirement expires in 2030.

OK, if that is clear as mud, likely you aren’t alone. Perhaps this will help.

Going back decades California voters have passed spending and taxing limits, which were placed in the state constitution. At the same time, they earmarked more and more of the state budget to be spent on specific state programs, such as education. That resulted in lawmakers having less flexibility with how and with what they can spend tax dollars.

Another budget dynamic is that state revenues are greatly dependent on income tax. When the economy is booming and high-earning Californians are making a killing on the stock market and their investments, the state is “flush” with cash.

But when the economy hiccups, the stock market falters and the high-rolling wealthy tax “cash cows,” decide to move to another state, California is left hurting for cash to pay the bills.

Just like a family, the state needs to have a “rainy day fund,” or more formally called a “budget stabilization account.”

“Putting money aside to protect ourselves from future uncertainties isn’t just good government; it’s common sense. California is strong and resilient, but we’re not immune to economic headwinds,” noted Gov. Gavin Newsom in support of Proposition 2. Newsom and Democratic legislative leaders contend having sufficient reserves is critical to California continuing to function in a system that depends heavily on a volatile source of revenue.

“At a time when our essential services are under pressure, we have a responsibility to safeguard the programs and investments that Californians rely on,” said Newsom.

While the Secretary of State did not receive an opposing ballot argument, the initiative does have its critics, including Assemblyman Joe Patterson, R-Rocklin, who contends raising the savings limit merely creates “a bigger slush fund.”

Also opposed are anti-tax groups, including Reform California and the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, which in its Voters Guide contended “California is in desperate need of fiscal discipline,” and Proposition 2 weakens spending and savings laws voters passed dating back to 1979.

Analyzing Proposition 2, the state Legislative Analyst Office concluded that “by continuing to require deposits into the rainy day fund until the balance reaches 20% of general fund taxes, the state would save more over time. In addition, by depositing more money when revenues are surging, the state likely would save more in the rainy day fund in certain years. More reserves would make balancing the budget somewhat easier when revenues decline.”

Vote YES on Proposition 2.
