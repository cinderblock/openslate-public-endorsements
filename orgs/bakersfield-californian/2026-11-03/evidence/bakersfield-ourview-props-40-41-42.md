Proposition 40 — “Imposes One-Time Tax on Certain Taxpayers.” (Commonly referred to as the California Billionaire Tax Act.)

Proposition 41 — “Prohibits New State Taxes That Exclude Revenues from State Spending Limit. Requires Audits for New State Special Taxes.” (Commonly referred to as the Taxpayer Protection and Accountability Act.)

Proposition 42 — “Prohibits New State Personal Property Taxes and Certain Retroactive State Taxes.” (Generally promoted as a “taxpayer protection measure” that bans new taxes on personal property and retroactive taxes.)

It’s best to consider all three propositions in the same editorial because one was placed on the ballot to ask voters to approve a new tax (40) and the other two (41 and 42) were placed on the ballot to fight it.

They all have one thing in common: They are bad tax schemes, with deceiving titles, that voters should reject.

### Proposition 40

Placed on the ballot by a healthcare labor union, Proposition 40 proposes to impose a one-time tax of 5% on the net worth (wealth) of individuals and trusts with more than $1 billion. Considered “wealth” would be businesses, stocks, securities, art, collectibles and intellectual property. Ninety percent of the money would go to healthcare; the remaining 10% would go to education, food assistance and tax administration. The one-time revenue would be exempted from some constitutional spending limits and school funding requirements.

Proponents claim Proposition 40 would raise about $100 billion from roughly 200 California billionaires. But the California Legislative Analyst’s Office predicts it will be much less because of the uncertainty over the value of taxable assets, who qualifies and the likelihood that some of the state’s wealthiest taxpayers would simply move out of California to avoid paying.

Proposition 40 was qualified for the ballot by the Service Employees International Union — United Healthcare Workers West (SEIU-UHW). The union has long used — or threatened to use — California ballot initiatives as leverage in its disputes with employers over labor contracts and public policies.

The union also qualified for this November’s ballot Proposition 44, which proposes to limit the executive salaries, administration and overhead costs of health centers serving medically underserved populations.

Voters may remember that in its ongoing organizing disputes with dialysis clinics, the union placed propositions on the ballot in 2018, 2020 and 2022. Voters overwhelmingly rejected all three.

In addition to the union, Proposition 40 is narrowly supported by the California Democratic Party, progressives, including U.S. Sen. Bernie Sanders, the Teamsters California, and the American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees (AFSCME).

In a split within labor and the Democratic Party, it is opposed by Gov. Gavin Newsom, as well as by the California Teachers Association, California Medical Association, California Primary Care Association, California Children’s Hospital Association, California Professional Firefighters, State Building and Construction Trades Council of California, California Conference of Carpenters, Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California, California Chamber of Commerce, California Taxpayers Association, California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce, California Asian Pacific Chamber of Commerce, and the California School Boards Association.

California voters also should oppose Proposition 40, which creates a one-time fund that does not solve the state’s health problems — escalating costs, facilities closures and millions losing their health insurance. It narrowly funnels tax revenues to limited beneficiaries, at the expense of other critical public programs. And it will do ongoing harm to the state’s economy by driving out some of its wealthiest residents and businesses.

### Proposition 41

Both Propositions 41 and 42 proponents reached beyond simply blocking Proposition 40, the Billionaires Tax, when they placed their initiatives on the ballot. They are proposing to greatly restrict Californians’ taxing ability today and into the future.

Proposition 41 would require the state auditor to audit the programs designated to receive new or increased statewide voter-proposed special taxes before an initiative could be placed on the ballot. The audits would be required to suggest ways recipient programs could cut costs. Programs receiving money from statewide special taxes would be audited every four years.

Proposition 41 would prohibit new state taxes enacted after Jan. 1, 2026 from being structured so their revenues are excluded from state spending limits.

The official Secretary of State record identifies the proponent of both Propositions 41 and 42 as Sacramento attorney Kurt R. Oneto, whose firm specializes in political, government, regulatory and litigation matters.

Both Propositions 41 and 42 are sponsored by Building a Better California, which is funded primarily by Google co-founder Sergey Brin, along with investors John Doerr and Michael Moritz. Other supporters are Reform California and the California Chamber of Commerce.

Assemblyman Carl DeMaio, R-San Diego, is the founder and chairman of Reform California, whose stated goal is to counter “far-left politicians and special interests,” and oppose taxes.

Voters should reject Proposition 41 not because it derails Proposition 40, but rather because it could make future voter-approved special tax initiatives extremely hard to qualify for the ballot. A big hurdle would be requiring the state auditor to audit recipient programs early in the signature gathering process — way before an initiative even qualifies for a ballot.

And audits don’t come for free. The Legislative Analyst’s Office estimates the time-consuming tax initiative audits would cost in the millions of dollars. The costs would be paid by the new tax revenues if the initiative passes. But if it never qualifies for the ballot, or voters reject it, audit costs likely would be paid from the state general fund — taxpayers.

Use of future special taxes also could be prevented by state spending limits enforced by Proposition 41.

### Proposition 42

Proposition 42 is the most direct assault on Proposition 40, the Billionaires Tax. It is a proposed constitutional amendment that would prevent new state taxes on personal property, including stocks, investment accounts, retirement accounts, business interests and intellectual property.

It also would prohibit retroactive taxation — the imposition of new taxes on activities, conduct or circumstances that occurred before the new tax became effective.

The official voter guide lists the principal supporters of Proposition 42 as California Professional Firefighters; State Building & Construction Trades Council of California; and the California branch of AMVETS, a veterans’ service organization.

Opponents include Bernie Sanders and SEIU–United Healthcare Workers West, the organization sponsoring Proposition 40.

A major concern is that Proposition 42 would restrict revenue options for future legislatures. While California currently does not tax ownership of most financial assets targeted by Proposition 40, the Legislative Analyst’s Office notes the state’s future tax-raising options would be blocked and that could reduce future state revenues, even to respond to emergencies.

Voters today should not place in the state’s constitution a permanent ban on an entire, broad category of potential taxes that may be necessary to meet California’s fiscal needs decades from now.
