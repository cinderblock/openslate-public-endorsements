Where CSEA Stands: Props 3, 39 and 43
September 16, 2026
As Californians prepare to vote in the November 3rd General Election, CSEA is taking positions on three statewide propositions that could have major impacts on classified employees, public education and the communities we serve.
Vote YES on Proposition 3
This is not a new tax. Prop 3 would keep existing income tax rates in place for the state’s highest earners to fund public education.
The already existing tax applies only to individuals earning more than $371,000 a year or households earning more than $740,000.
CSEA has endorsed a YES vote on Prop 3 because it would protect an important source of funding that California schools and community colleges have relied on for over a decade. CSEA members understand the importance of keeping this existing tax to fund their jobs and played an important role in getting Prop 3 on the ballot by collecting more than 30,000 petition signatures earlier this year.
If Prop 3 fails, California schools and community colleges would lose between $5 billion and $15 billion a year once the rates expire. That would mean less money for programs, services and other resources our schools rely on, including money that pays the wages of school employees like you.
The passage of Prop 3 would continue directing that revenue to public education, with requirements in place to ensure funding goes straight to local schools and community colleges. Sacramento politicians cannot touch it or redirect it, not one penny may be spent on bureaucracy or administrative overhead, every dollar is independently audited every year, and districts must post their spending online.
Prop 3 impacts CSEA members like you because school funding directly affects the resources districts have available to support classified jobs and the services you provide every day.
You can see how much money your district currently receives from the existing Prop 3 wealth tax by using the searchable K-12 school district database at csea.com/props. Simply enter the name of your school district to see an estimate of the annual funding your district could lose if Prop 3 does not pass. The amount varies from district to district, and the database provides a clearer picture of what could be at stake close to home.
Vote NO on Proposition 39
CSEA opposes Prop 39 because it would expose California voters' personal information and make it more difficult to vote.
Prop 39 would require you to write the last four digits of your government-issued ID on the outside of your ballot envelope, exposing you to identity theft risk. It would also require you to show government-issued ID before your ballot could be counted when voting in person.
CSEA opposes the measure because these added requirements could make voting more difficult for some eligible voters by requiring them to expose personal information when casting their ballots.
Prop 39 also comes with significant costs to taxpayers. According to the California Legislative Analyst’s Office, the state estimates that implementing the new requirements could cost state and local governments tens to hundreds of millions of dollars each year.
Advocates of Prop 39 cite fraud and corruption in elections, however the California Attorney General’s investigations have found no evidence of either. Even the Heritage Foundation, an ultra-conservative organization, has identified only 71 confirmed cases of voter fraud in California from 1982 to 2025.
Vote NO on Proposition 43
CSEA also opposes Prop 43, which would make it harder for you to fund critical services in your community like police and fire departments, fix and repair local roads, support local hospitals, and staff our local schools, directly threatening your job as a school employee.
In America, we believe in one person, one vote, and the majority wins. Prop. 43 throws that out. It empowers just over one-third of voters to block what nearly 67% want. It puts the out-voted minority in charge, even if they are extremists. It makes their NO vote count twice as much as a YES vote.
