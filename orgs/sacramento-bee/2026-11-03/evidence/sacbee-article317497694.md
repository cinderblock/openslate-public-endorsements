Sacramento City Unified’s fiscal failures make Measure M a ballot stink bomb | Opinion

By The Sacramento Bee Editorial Board

October 5, 2026 11:30 AM

The Sacramento City Unified School District logo at the Serna Center in Sacramento on Thursday, Feb. 5, 2026.

JOSÉ LUIS VILLEGAS

jvillegas@sacbee.com

The Sacramento City Unified School District remains mired in a fiscal crisis. In June, the district approved a budget projecting a $228.9 million deficit by the end of the 2026-27 school year. In September, the district revised its budget projections to a $162,000 surplus for the same period.

As The Bee’s Chaewon Chung reported, “Michael Fine, CEO of the state-funded Fiscal Crisis and Management Assistance Team, described the district’s projection as ‘absurd,’ saying it did not meet California school-district budgeting standards and pointing to what he called unreasonable assumptions about future funding.”

Fine said, “no other district in the state that would count that.”

That is an extraordinarily shaky foundation on which to ask taxpayers for an open-ended stream of money.

Yet that is what the district is doing with Measure M, a $95-a-year parcel tax to raise about $10 million annually for special education and early intervention. The tax could increase with inflation, and it would continue until voters repeal or replace it.

Clearly, district students need help. But district leaders first must demonstrate that they can put Sacramento’s schools on firm financial footing before voters entrust them with another tax. We hoped district leaders would provide us with straight answers during our meeting with them. They did not.

This is why The Bee Editorial Board recommends a “No” vote on Measure M.

When we asked for a specific plan showing how the roughly $10 million generated by Measure M would be spent, district officials couldn’t produce one. They could describe potential uses — more social workers, psychologists and nurses, professional development and staff time for intervention teams — but could not provide a detailed allocation.

District leaders also could not explain why they had not sought concessions from the teachers union and other labor partners to help balance the district’s books. Instead, they are asking the public for more money without demonstrating that they can be trusted to make sound fiscal decisions.

The other concern is the district’s mixed record in managing its special education programs.

An independent monitor found that Black students were identified for special education at a 29.4% rate, compared with 18.7% districtwide and 15% statewide. The report found weaknesses in the general education interventions intended to help students before special education becomes necessary.

The monitor exists because of a lawsuit filed by the Black Parallel School Board and individual students that alleged discrimination against students with disabilities, particularly Black students. A May 2023 settlement established independent monitoring and a process for a five-year action plan.

District officials have relied in part on a framework known as a multi-tiered system of supports, or MTSS. Students struggling with reading, behavior, emotional well-being or attendance would receive help early, with more intensive assistance added as needed. The goal is to address needs in general education when appropriate while promptly identifying and assessing students who may require special education.

While the district has seen some encouraging results at some schools, the independent monitor found the district’s MTSS implementation remains fragmented. A lack of common procedures, assessments and documentation made it difficult to consistently track what interventions students have received, whether they worked and what help should come next. The district didn’t reliably maintain student interventions in a centralized system, creating problems for children who transitioned to different schools.

District officials meaningful student outcomes could take three to five years or longer to emerge.

That makes the structure of Measure M especially critical.

Voters should insist that the district release key program metrics at regular intervals and establish in advance the results that would constitute success. The district should publish a detailed plan for expanding MTSS and report regularly on whether the program is improving student outcomes. The board and district leadership must also restructure the district’s finances to restore long-term stability

After the district meets these tests, its leaders can return to voters with a measure that includes firm accountability provisions. Until then, we recommend a “No” vote on Measure M.
