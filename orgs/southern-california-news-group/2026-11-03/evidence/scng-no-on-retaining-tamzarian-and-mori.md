PUBLISHED: October 7, 2026 at 11:52 PM PDT

State judges in California, like all public officials, take an oath [https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&sectionNum=SEC.%203.&article=XX] when they are sworn into office. They swear or affirm that they will “support and defend” the federal and state constitutions and “bear true faith and allegiance to them.”

Yet in recent years, state judges have interpreted away an important protection that the people of California wrote into their constitution with 1978’s Proposition 13 and 1996’s Proposition 218 – the provision [https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/constitution-of-the-state-of-california-1879/ca-const-art-xiiia-sect-4/] stating that local governments may impose “special” taxes “by a two-thirds vote” of the local electorate, “except” two particular types of taxes. One of those is “a transaction tax or sales tax on the sale of real property.”

Last December, the Second District Court of Appeal upheld Measure ULA, the so-called “mansion tax” in Los Angeles, even though it is a “transaction tax or sales tax on the sale of real property” for a “special” purpose that did not receive “a two-thirds vote.” The court asserted that “the voters did not generally intend for Proposition 13 to limit their own power to raise taxes by initiative.”

The opinion [https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2025/b334071.html] was written by Justice Armen Tamzarian and signed by Justice Audra Mori. Appointed by Gov. Gavin Newsom in 2025 and 2023, respectively, both are seeking the approval of voters to continue on the court for a 12-year term. They are on the ballot in Los Angeles, Ventura, Santa Barbara and San Luis Obispo counties.

Voters should vote “No” to both to send a strong message that constitutional taxpayer protections may not be erased by judicial opinions.

In a series of cases, appellate courts have cited ambiguous language in a 2017 California Supreme Court decision, California Cannabis Coalition v. City of Upland, to hold that the two-thirds vote requirement for special (earmarked) taxes does not apply to tax increases proposed by citizens’ initiatives. Tamzarian and Mori went further, holding that the “except” clause barring real estate transfer taxes doesn’t apply to citizens’ initiatives, either.

The courts have not even cared that many of these “citizens’ initiatives” were actually initiated by government officials. The campaign to collect signatures for a June 2018 initiative tax increase in San Francisco was run by two of the San Francisco County supervisors. Proposition C, a special tax, passed with 50.8% of the vote and was upheld by an appellate court.

The California Supreme Court has refused to review any of the appellate decisions inventing this constitutional workaround and has never overruled the 1987 case, Altadena Library District v. Bloodgood [https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/192/585.html], which applied the two-thirds vote requirement to a citizens’ initiative just as it would a local government body.

Suspiciously, the court showed no equivalent deference to citizens’ initiatives when it tossed the 2024 Taxpayer Protection and Government Accountability Act from the ballot or when it invalidated a 2012 pension reform measure in San Diego in 2018.

In this election, voters have an opportunity to tell the courts that democracy is not a one-way street leading to higher taxes and more government spending. Vote yes on Proposition 43 to restore the two-thirds vote requirement for local special taxes, and vote “No” to retaining Tamzarian and Mori.
