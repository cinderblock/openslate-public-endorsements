By Mercury News East Bay Times Editorial Boards
PUBLISHED: October 16, 2026 at 12:46 AM PDT | UPDATED: October 19, 2026 at 10:17 AM PDT
Getting your Trinity Audio player ready...
It’s impossible not to view Proposition 39 — the measure authored by Republican Assemblymember Carl DeMaio and backed by out-of-state, right-wing billionaires — strictly through the lens of national politics.
In requiring voters to show IDs before receiving their ballots (among other restrictive voting rules and audits), it gives credence to President Donald Trump’s longstanding and repeatedly debunked claims that millions of noncitizens routinely vote in U.S. elections.
This initiative is an expensive solution to a phantom problem. Reject Prop. 39.
Manufactured crisis
Multiple groups have studied the issue and found scant evidence of widespread fraud. The California State Library looked at 14 counties where grand juries issued reports on the risk of voter fraud in the 2020, 2022 and 2024 elections and found secure elections everywhere. The Brennan Center for Justice’s research confirmed that voter fraud is a myth. Even the conservative Heritage Foundation could only find 71 instances of voter fraud in California — over more than four decades. Also, California already takes election integrity seriously, which is one reason our process of confirming elections is so slow.
Our editorial board repeatedly asked the Yes on Prop. 39 campaign for evidence of voter fraud in California, rampant or otherwise, and they deflected, dodged and denied the premise of our questions. They insisted that fraud wasn’t actually the issue — that Prop. 39 is about improving public trust and confidence in our democracy.
So it’s purely coincidence that Donald Trump demanded “voter ID” laws in California as a condition for wildfire disaster relief last year. Or that the measure’s biggest backer, Illinois billionaire Richard Uihlein, has a history of contributing to the campaigns of election deniers.
According to the Public Policy Institute of California, a quarter of likely voters are very concerned that it’s too easy for ineligible people to vote. That’s not zero, but it also doesn’t mean we should be changing election laws to accommodate the fact-free fears of a minority of Californians.
In other polls about trust in our elections, it is overwhelmingly Republicans who are the most concerned. That’s fine. But if they really cared about improving those numbers, we’d recommend they start by asking the president to stop spreading false information.
We absolutely wouldn’t recommend, as Prop. 39 does, that private citizens be allowed to sue if they believe the government isn’t complying with the measure’s rules. We don’t even want to imagine the kind of frivolous litigation that could inspire.
Why panic?
It’s possible to view Prop. 39 and say, “What’s the big deal?” Thirty-six states require voters to present identification before voting in person.
Well, the big deal is that a lot of eligible people might not be able to vote if the measure is passed. Millions of Californians wouldn’t have the ID required to satisfy this proposal. (They’d have to request a new one.) And, as the No on Prop. 39 campaign pointed out to us, when Texas implemented similar mail-ballot ID rules, rejections shot up from 1% to 12%.
Plus, completely revamping the voter verification system will come with financial costs — “between tens of millions of dollars and low hundreds of millions of dollars each year,” according to the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst’s Office.
Prop. 39 isn’t just about voter ID. It contains a provision that is deeply problematic: requiring election officials to use their “best efforts” to verify the citizenship status of voters they register, with regular audits to ensure compliance.
But there is no reliable government list of U.S. citizens. The Supreme Court decided that the federal government could continue to try to create one, but it’s already full of mistakes, labeling many citizens as ineligible to vote.
This deeply flawed measure would create chaos for no reason.
Reject Prop. 39.
