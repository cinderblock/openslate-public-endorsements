Props. 1 and 38 would take the state deeper in debt for uncertain gains
Two measures on the November ballot ask voters to borrow $20 billion combined. Proposition 1 would fund housing programs. Proposition 38 would fund immunology research. We recommend a no vote on each.
The state already carries a heavy debt load. California owed about $70 billion on general obligation bonds as of Sept. 1, according to the state treasurer. Voters and lawmakers have authorized nearly $38 billion more in bonds that have not yet been sold.
The bondholders who lent the state money expect to be paid back with interest. The state spends about $6 billion annually from the general fund repaying bonds. Props. 1 and 38 would add roughly $1 billion to that for 20 to 25 years.
When state revenue comes up short, lawmakers can reduce spending or at least slow spending growth. Bond payments, however, are a legal obligation that comes due regardless. Every dollar locked into debt service is a taxpayer dollar unavailable for schools, health care or wildfire protection in a lean year.
Prop. 1: Housing money without accountability
California needs more housing, and Prop. 1 would throw $11.25 billion at the problem without addressing the underlying causes of the state’s housing shortage and high costs. Lawmakers took the easy route. Rather than pass controversial reforms or find money in the budget, they chose to ask voters to borrow.
Most of the money, about $7.2 billion, would pay for building, buying and rehabilitating multifamily rental housing, affordable housing and supportive housing for the homeless. Smaller pools of money totaling about $2.3 billion would provide mortgage and down-payment assistance.
Giving buyers more money with which to bid without adding homes would push prices higher. And buying existing apartments does not add a single home to ease market pressure.
The measure also lacks accountability. It calls for an annual report on how well things are going, but there is no independent audit on most of it and no remedy if the return on investment is lackluster. There also are no plans for when the money runs out and no guarantee that lawmakers will not meddle and reapportion the funds.
Californians have cause for skepticism that the state will spend this housing money well. A 2024 state audit found that California had not consistently tracked whether its homelessness spending worked. Sacramento should prove that it can measure and deliver results before asking for bonds that will cost taxpayers $500 million to $600 million per year.
Prop. 38: Science by ballot box
Immunology and immunotherapy hold real promise, but Prop. 38 is the wrong way to fund research into them. Only one University of California-affiliated institute appears to qualify for half of the money, according to a CalMatters analysis. Coincidentally, that institute’s co-founder is one of Prop. 38’s biggest backers.
One problem with this approach is that state law would lock in how the money could be spent. Research dollars should support the centers with the most promising results and best scientists. Maybe that is the UC affiliate now, but that could change.
Bonds also fit research poorly. They are better suited to bridges and schools that last decades than to research that might never bear fruit. Even if there are commercial breakthroughs, the Legislative Analyst’s Office says royalties could take decades to repay the bonds, if they ever do.
Medical research is a legitimate state interest. If California wants to fund it, the state ought to do so through the regular budget process, weighing its value against other state priorities, not with a special bond that will cost taxpayers $500 million to $600 million per year.
